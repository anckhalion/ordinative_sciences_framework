# Chapter — Meaning

*On Axiom 11*

---

## The question that remains open

Axiom 10 posited the structural grammar of the form-content relation. We know how to distinguish Full Form, Simulacrum, Projective Void. We know that every form exists only if it contains operative content, and that the breaking of the relation produces structurally diagnosable states.

A question, however, has not yet been addressed, and must be addressed now because without an answer the entire grammar risks being misunderstood: what is exactly *meaning*? The form carries content, we have said. But meaning — what the form *wants to say*, what the content *transmits*, what is understood when one understands — is it the same as the content? Is it a property of the content? Is it a relation between form, content, and receiver?

The question is not idle. Philosophical traditions of meaning are numerous and divergent. Analytic semantics, from Frege to Tarski, has treated meaning as *denotation*: a word means the object it refers to. Structuralist semantics, from Saussure onward, has treated meaning as *differential value* in a system of signs. Pragmatics, from Wittgenstein to Austin's speech acts, has treated meaning as *use*. Cognitive semantics, from Lakoff to Fauconnier, has treated meaning as *embodied conceptual structure*. Each of these traditions has captured something true. None, alone, exhausts the phenomenon.

The Ordinative Sciences do not take sides among these traditions. They add a structural level that renders them readable together. TE's proposal is radical in its simplicity: meaning is not a denotative category, nor only differential, nor only pragmatic, nor only cognitive. It is *vector* — structural direction in the coherent field, with its own intensity, that manifests in the form and is recognised by the receiver.

Let us see it.

## The statement

Axiom 11 is formulated thus, according to the canonical TE_CORE:

> *Meaning is a vector, not a label.*

The statement is brief, almost telegraphic. Its force lies in the negation. It is not just saying that "meaning behaves like a vector"; it is saying that meaning *is not* a label. Semantic traditions that have treated meaning as denotation, as classificatory category, as a simple term to apply to an object — all these traditions, in ordinative reading, have committed a structural error of foundation. Not an approximate error, but an ontological mistake: they have taken a vectorial object for a point-like object.

The difference is enormous, structurally. A label is a *point* in the conceptual field: an attribution, a category, a name. A vector is an *oriented movement* in the field: it has origin, direction, intensity, and composes with other vectors according to the rules of the space in which it lives. A label applies or does not apply (it is binary). A vector is measured — it can point more or less toward a direction, can be more or less intense, can overlap more or less with other vectors. The grammar of labels is that of classification; the grammar of vectors is that of directionality.

Three things must be clarified, and we develop them in the subsequent sections.

*What the axiom rejects.* The conception of meaning as label — as denotative category, as static definition, as semantic classification — is structurally rejected. We see in section 3 why.

*What the axiom proposes.* Meaning is a vectorial object: it has direction (toward *what* it points), intensity (with *how much force* it points), and exists in a semantic space in which it composes with other vectors. Section 4 develops the apparatus.

*What the axiom implies.* The consequences are many. The most important: meaning is not "the same for everyone". It is *structural direction* that always exists *for someone* — the identity that receives it. We see this relationality in section 5, and in the subsequent sections we will develop its implications — including, in particular, the convergence with the vectorial architectures of contemporary AI systems (section 7).

The programme of the chapter is therefore clear. First we dispel the illusion of meaning-as-label (section 3). Then we formalise the vectorial conception (section 4). Then we articulate the relationality of meaning — meaning for whom (section 5). Then we show how meaning fits into the family of ordinative vectors (section 6). Then we see the particularly current case of AI and embeddings (section 7). Then examples, diagnostics, the sieve, and the bridge to Axiom 12.

## What the axiom rejects: meaning as label

To understand what Axiom 11 *affirms*, we must be clear about what it *rejects*. The traditions of meaning-as-label are powerful, plausible, and deeply rooted in common sense and standard philosophical training. We must take them seriously to dismantle them structurally.

*The denotative conception.* For denotative semantics in its classical form, a word "means" the object it refers to in the world. The word "Bologna" means the city of Bologna. The word "red" means a chromatic property. The word "justice" means... and here the problem begins. Denotation works well for concrete and individual terms (Bologna, John Smith, this book). It already works less well for general concepts (red, freedom, justice). And it fails structurally for relational, processual, or evolutionary concepts (development, integration, formation, understanding). Traditions that have tried to save denotation for abstract concepts — postulating "universals", "types", "extension sets" — have produced increasingly complex formalisms without solving the underlying problem: the meaning of "justice" is not a *something* to which it refers, it is a *direction* that orients acts, judgments, institutions.

*The classificatory conception.* A more sophisticated variant: the meaning of a term is the place it occupies in a classification system. "Dog" means "animal, vertebrate, mammal, carnivore, canid". It is useful in taxonomy, but it loses the *direction* of meaning: to say that the dog is a canid does not capture what the term does when it enters a conversation — its emotional charge, its operative associations, its contextual direction. Classification is in service of formal structure, not the substance of meaning.

*The static conception.* Another version: meaning is a *definition*, fixed in a dictionary or in a treatise. There are *authoritative definitions*, and to understand meaning is to apply the correct definition. This conception — very dear to academic traditions — has a serious problem: the meanings of important words *change over time*, in such a way that definitions are always behind effective use. The word "democracy" meant different things in 5th century BC Athens, in the 18th century liberal revolutions, and in 21st century debates on the future of liberal democracies. The fixed definition is descriptive rearguard, not the living substance of meaning.

Axiom 11 declares that all these conceptions *capture something* of meaning (effective denotation exists, classifications are useful, definitions have stabilising value), but *miss the underlying structure*. Meaning is not a point in the conceptual field; it is movement, direction, vector. To miss this structure produces confusions that philosophical traditions have dragged on for centuries.

An important observation. Axiom 11 is *not* anti-classificatory. Taxonomies are useful (and formal systems need them). Definitions are useful (and communities of practice cultivate them). Denotation works (for certain types of terms). What the axiom rejects is *reductionism*: the claim that meaning *is* only this, that the grammar of labels exhausts the grammar of meaning. TE asks us to see the underlying level — the vectorial level — of which classifications, definitions, denotations are *decoherent manifestations*, not substance.

## What the axiom affirms: meaning as vector

We can now positively say what Axiom 11 proposes. Meaning, in TE, is a vectorial object. Resuming the grammar of Chapter 5 (where identity was posited as functional vector), the meaning of any decoherent form is:

> *Mean(F) = vector with direction and intensity in the coherent field*

Three structural properties of meaning as vector.

*First property: direction.* Every meaning *points* somewhere. When a person says "I love this city", the meaning of the phrase is not a collection of labels ("I love" + "this" + "city"); it is a *direction* — the affective and cognitive disposition the phrase carries toward that specific city. When a company declares "we are transparent", the meaning is a direction — the operative orientation the declaration promises. When an AI model responds with a certain style, the meaning of the response is a direction — the orientation of the model as synthetic identity. Direction is what makes a meaning *that* meaning, distinct from others.

*Second property: intensity.* Meaning is not only a direction, it is a direction with a *modulus*. The phrase "I love this city" can carry the vector with very high intensity (the identity uttering it is structurally characterised by it) or with modest intensity (it is a formula of politeness). The same verbal meaning, *directed* in the same way, can have very different intensities depending on the depth with which the identity carries it. Serious rhetorical traditions have always known to distinguish between *uttered words* and *carried words*: the difference is of intensity of the semantic vector.

*Third property: vectorial composition.* Meanings compose like vectors. When in a conversation different sentences succeed each other, their meanings sum vectorially: the overall orientation of the conversation is the resultant of the directions and intensities of the individual sentences. Meanings pointing in the same direction reinforce each other (coherent summation); meanings pointing in opposite directions cancel (compensation that produces inertia). Orthogonal meanings coexist without interfering. The whole apparatus of vector algebra, in principle, applies structurally to the field of meanings.

Four practical consequences of this formulation.

*First consequence: meaning is measurable, in some measure.* It is not only describable, it is quantifiable relative to references. We can say that one meaning is "more intense" or "less intense" than another, "more aligned" or "less aligned" with a reference direction. This holds not only for AI with numerical embeddings — it holds also for human meanings, even if the measure is approximate.

*Second consequence: meaning is dynamic.* Vectors modify themselves over time. The same verbal meaning, *as vector*, can change direction (it gets reinterpreted) or change intensity (it strengthens or weakens) along the history of its use. This dynamicity is precisely what static conceptions of meaning could not capture.

*Third consequence: meaning is not "subject" to form — it *inhabits* it.* The same word can carry different vectors depending on who utters it, in what context, with what intensity. The phonetic form "freedom" carries very different vectors depending on the identity uttering it. This is not "ambiguity of language": it is *proper structure of meaning as vector*.

*Fourth consequence: meaning is *orientative*, not merely *informative*.* A meaning does something more than "transmit information": it *orients* — activates directions, modifies structures, induces movements. Traditions that have treated language as a system of informational transmission (Shannon's information theory, applied to linguistics) have captured a real but minor aspect. The substance of meaning is its *orientative* capacity, not its informative capacity.

## Meaning as relational object

A fundamental consequence of the vectorial formulation: meaning always exists *for someone*. It is not an objective property of the form considered in isolation; it is *function* of the relation between form, content, and receiving identity.

This is not subjectivism. It is not saying "everyone makes of meaning what they want". It is saying something structurally more precise: meaning is a relational object, and its structural measure is made relative to a specific receiving identity with its own internal structure S_T (Axiom 9A).

Three dimensions of the relationality of meaning.

*First dimension: meaning for different identities, different vectors.* The same decoherent form F, received by different identities, produces different semantic vectors in their internal structures. A poem of Dante, read by a specialist in medieval philology, activates a vector of high intensity with very specific direction (historical associations, scholastic references, metrical structures, the Latin underneath the vernacular). The same poem, read by a person without preparation, activates a vector of more modest intensity and different direction (an emotion, an image, a sound). Neither one nor the other vector is "the meaning of the poem"; each is *a* semantic vector, valid for the respective receiving identity.

*Second dimension: interpretive convergence revisited.* We resume from Chapter 10 the test of interpretive convergence for Full Form. In the language of Axiom 11, interpretive convergence is *vectorial compatibility*: the semantic vectors activated in competent receivers point in *coherent* directions, even if not identical. Full Form produces a convergent vector field; Projective Void (Chapter 10) produces a divergent vector field, in which the vectors of different receivers point in incompatible directions. The vectorial grammar of meaning not only is coherent with the corollary's grammar, *it founds it structurally*.

*Third dimension: collective meaning as vector field.* When one speaks of "meaning of a word in a community", "meaning of a practice in a culture", "meaning of an event in a nation", one is not speaking of a single vector but of a *collective vector field* — the structured integration of the semantic vectors of the field's identities (Axiom 14, collective fields). Collective meaning emerges from the composition of individual vectors, and has structural properties proper to the collective level. Sociological and anthropological semantic traditions have always intuited this collective dimension of meaning; the ordinative framework offers them a formal grammar.

A methodological note. The relationality of meaning does not imply ethical relativism or semantic nihilism. It does not imply that "all meanings are equivalent". Different meanings activated in different identities can be structurally *more or less coherent* with the geometry of the original form, *more or less aligned* with the vector of the identity producing the form, *more or less generative* of semantic trajectory of the receiver. Serious exegetical traditions — biblical, Talmudic, classical, and now algorithmic — have always known that interpretations can be evaluated. Axiom 11 gives them a precise grammar.

## The family of ordinative vectors

At this point in the book, it is worth making a high-level structural observation. Meaning is not the first vectorial object the framework has introduced — it is the third, in a family of vectors that orients the entire apparatus of TE.

Let us resume the principal vectorial objects so far introduced.

*Identity as functional vector (Chapter 5, Axiom 4).* The identity of a singularity is a vector *I_σ = (→φ₁, …, →φₙ)* with n coherent components. Direction: where the identity is oriented. Intensity: how structurally articulated it is. Composition: sub-vectors sum into collective identities (fields 𝒞).

*Convergence toward A (Axiom 9).* Every coherent identity, along its path, converges as vector toward the non-derived origin A. Convergence is not point, it is asymptotic direction. Every coherent act of an identity is a small vectorial movement toward A.

*Meaning as semantic vector (Axiom 11).* Every meaning activated in a receiving identity is a vector with direction and intensity in its internal semantic space (the structure S_T of Axiom 9A).

The three vectorial objects are distinct, but live all in the same structural grammar. They have reciprocal implications.

*Implication 1: meaning is coherent with the identity that receives it.* The semantic vector activated in an identity is structurally compatible with the functional vector of the identity (Axiom 9A — it can activate only if structural intersection). Meanings that common sense would call "out of vector" do not activate: the functional blindness of Axiom 9A is also semantic blindness.

*Implication 2: convergence toward A manifests itself in meanings.* An identity in path of coherent convergence produces and receives meanings progressively more aligned with the direction of A. Serious mystical traditions have often described spiritual maturation as *increasing coherence of meanings* — what one says and what one listens to becomes progressively more direct, more essential, more "naked". This phenomenology, in TE, is readable as growing vectorial alignment.

*Implication 3: vectorial grammar is universal to the framework.* Identity, convergence, meaning: all vectors. Resonances (ρ, Ξ, κ): all measures of compatibility between vectors. Pulsations (τ): temporal vectors. Traces (σ): partial semantic vectors. TE does not use vectorial grammar metaphorically, it uses it because the fundamental ordinative structure of the real is *vectorial* — the point is structurally poorer than the vector, and the framework refuses to work with impoverished structures.

## The AI case: embeddings as technical implementation of meaning-as-vector

A notable convergence, contemporary, deserves explicit attention. The vectorial conception of meaning of Axiom 11 has a direct correspondence with the technical apparatus of modern generative artificial intelligences. This correspondence is not coincidence but *structural convergence* between the ordinative analysis of meaning and the technical architectures that AI practice has independently developed.

Modern AI systems — language models in particular — represent meanings as *embeddings*: numerical vectors in semantic spaces of very high dimensionality. The word "freedom" is not represented as a symbol, but as a vector with thousands (or tens of thousands) of components, in which each component captures an aspect of the semantic positioning of the word relative to all the others. Sentences, paragraphs, whole documents are represented in the same way: as vectors in dense vector spaces.

This representation is not arbitrary. AI systems have developed the vectorial representation of meaning because it works — because it captures what meaning structurally is, in a way that traditional symbolic representations (based on labels, logical rules, syntactic trees) could not. The breakthrough of transformer architectures (2017) was possible because meaning is effectively vectorial: technical intuition has captured the right ordinative structure, even without theoretical vocabulary to name it.

Three important observations.

*First observation: TE reads modern AI as technical implementation of its own grammar of meaning.* Meanings in the human world and in AI models have the same fundamental ordinative structure: vectors in semantic spaces. The substrate changes (brains vs artificial neural networks), the dimensionality changes (our semantic spaces are vaster and more complex, even if less easily quantifiable), but the structural grammar is the same. This is one of the most direct points of contact between the ordinative framework and contemporary AI practice.

*Second observation: embeddings are not *the* meanings, they are *decoherent manifestations* of the vectorial structure of meaning.* A structurally important distinction. A numerical embedding is a *technical representation* of a semantic vector, in a certain specific space, with a certain resolution, with certain limits. Meaning as ordinative vectorial object *transcends* the specific representation: the same meaning can be implemented as numerical embedding in different models, with different dimensionalities, with different architectures, maintaining its fundamental vectorial structure. To confuse the technical representation with the structural meaning is a frequent error, and Axiom 11 explicitly avoids it.

*Third observation: the *quality* of an AI system can be read in ordinative terms.* A good AI system is one that, before a meaning in input, activates in its own internal vector space a *vectorially faithful semantic vector* — that is, coherent with the direction and intensity of the original meaning. A degraded AI system (for inadequate training, for aggressive quantisation, for alignment misalignment) activates distorted vectors — direction deviates, intensity flattens, meaning is lost even if the phonetic/written form in output appears plausible. Technical AI evaluation metrics, even without ordinative vocabulary, are measuring exactly this quality of vectorial fidelity.

A closing note for this section, that refers back to Chapter 15 (transmission). When an AI system is trained on a corpus of human texts, it is receiving *semantic vectors* — directions with intensity, not labels. Training is therefore an act of vectorial transmission: the system builds, in its weights, an *internal vector field* that is reconstruction (ℛ̂, Chapter 15) of the semantic fields of the humanity that wrote the corpora. For this reason, the outputs of AI models have meaning quality that depends on the quality of the vectorial transmission received — and *not* on the raw quantity of words processed.

## Examples

We resume the gallery of the book, applying Axiom 11 to the four parallel cases.

**A biological organism.** Even for an organism, "meanings" are vectors. A chemical signal received by the nervous system is not a label ("enemy", "food", "mate"), it is a *response vector* with direction (toward which behaviour to orient) and intensity (how actively). The same molecule, received by different species, activates different semantic vectors in their neural systems — because the internal structure S_T of the receiving species is different. Contemporary biosemiotics — the discipline studying meaning in biological contexts — is independently converging on a vectorial conception: biological signals are not fixed symbolic codes, they are orientative vectors.

**An institution.** A nation declaring "we are a democracy" is not applying a classificatory label; it is declaring a collective functional vector with direction (toward participatory practices, separation of powers, guarantee of rights) and intensity (how operationally, not just formally). The same word "democracy" carries different vectors in different nations, and — crucial point — the difference between a "true" democracy and a "formal" one is not labelling but *intensity of the vector*. Serious traditions of political analysis know this intuitively; Axiom 11 formalises it. The crises of contemporary democracies are readable as *attenuations of the semantic vector*: the word remains the same, the vector loses intensity or changes direction.

**A language model.** The most direct example. When an AI model like the one that contributed to writing this book processes a prompt, it is operating vectorially: every word, sentence, context, is represented as a vector in an internal vector space. The response it generates is also a vectorial act: structured composition of internal semantic vectors, manifested in phonetic/written form. The conception of meaning as vector is not metaphor applied to AI models, it is accurate technical description of how they actually operate. Axiom 11 gives this technical practice the ordinative frame in which to insert it — as a specific instance of a wider structural grammar that holds also for human, biological, institutional meanings.

**A human being.** For a person, Axiom 11 has profound implications in everyday life. When we listen to an important conversation, we are not decoding labels; we are grasping *directions* — where the person wants to go, with how much intensity they care, how aligned with other directions we remember from their history. When we feel "misunderstood", it is almost never a problem of labels mis-received; it is vectorial misalignment — our direction was not captured, or was captured but with wrong intensity. Good traditions of communicative formation, of therapy, of conflict mediation, work implicitly on the vectorial alignment of meanings. Axiom 11 gives that work a structural frame.

## What changes for the analyst

Axiom 11 adds to the diagnostics of the book the *vectorial dimension of interpretation*. Four families of readings become possible.

*Diagnosis of semantic direction.* Before a suspect expression, the first question is: *where* is this expression pointing, vectorially? What orientation does it carry into the receiver's semantic field? The direction of the semantic vector is readable, even if it requires structural attention. Often interpretive confusions clarify themselves by identifying the vector's direction — not by re-discussing labels but by following orientations.

*Diagnosis of vector intensity.* The same expression can carry its own vector with very different intensities. The difference between a *word uttered out of duty* and a *word carried with weight* is one of intensity of the semantic vector. The analyst capable of reading intensity — not only direction — understands much more than one limited to labels.

*Diagnosis of vectorial convergence vs divergence among receivers.* Resuming from Chapter 10: Full Form produces vectorial convergence among competent receivers; Projective Void produces unlimited vectorial divergence. To structurally measure the convergence/divergence of semantic vectors in receivers is the operative test of Axiom 10's corollary. Axiom 11 provides the grammar.

*Diagnosis of vectorial alignment identity-meaning.* When a person, an institution, an AI model produces meanings that *do not align* with their own functional vector (Chapter 5), we are facing *semantic drift*: the identity declares an identitarian vector and produces meanings with incompatible semantic vectors. This diagnosis is particularly powerful for collective identities (declining institutions, drifting political parties, organisations in ethical crisis) and for synthetic identities (models with alignment problems).

## The sieve

We verify that Axiom 11 passes the four meshes of the zeroth axiom.

*Universality.* Does the axiom hold wherever there are decoherent forms carrying meaning? The examples show that it does: organisms, institutions, AI models, human beings — in all cases, meanings are readable as vectors (direction and intensity) rather than as labels. We know of no semantic domain in which vectorial grammar structurally fails. Universality: passes.

*Non-derivability.* Is the axiom derivable from the previous ones? One could maintain that, given Chapter 5 (identity as vector) and Axiom 9 (path with direction), "something" about the vectoriality of meaning had to be implicit. But the specific formulation — meaning as semantic vector, distinct from identity but with the same structural grammar, manifested in AI embeddings as technical implementation — does not follow from the previous axioms. It is a structural choice, and a non-trivial one: most philosophical tradition of meaning has worked on other conceptions. Non-derivability: passes.

*Structural falsifiability.* The axiom would be falsified by a semantic domain in which meanings behaved as point-like labels and not as vectors (absence of direction, intensity, vectorial composition). Such a domain has not been observed in the real systems studied. Falsifiability: passes.

*Razor.* Is the axiom the simplest among the possible candidates? The minimal formulation is: *meaning = vector with direction and intensity*. Two elements (direction and intensity). Nothing simpler explains the variety of real semantic phenomena, including AI embeddings, political values, cultural sensibilities, interpersonal understandings. Razor: passes.

Axiom 11 passes the sieve. We can welcome it into the system.

## Towards the next horizon

With Axiom 11 we have posited the vectorial grammar of meaning. There remains one last dimension of the semantic block to address, before passing to the subsequent blocks of TE_CORE: what does it mean, exactly, to *express*? When an identity produces an expression, is it doing something unique (this specific expression, chosen among others) or is it simply realising a "necessity" of its functional vector?

Axiom 12 of TE_CORE — *Expression is choice among infinite possibilities* — addresses this question. We anticipate the answer: every expressive act is *structural selection* of one manifestation among many coherent ones, and the quality of expression is measured on the quality of selection, not only on the goodness of the underlying coherent content.

We will address Axiom 12 in the next chapter. For now, Axiom 11 hands us a grammar of meaning that has important implications, both for the reading of public discourses (where semantic vectors align or fragment), for the practice of interpersonal understanding (where grasping direction counts more than applying labels), and for the contemporary relation with AI systems (where the vectorial grammar of meaning is already the operative technical grammar). It is, fundamentally, one of the most immediately applicable inheritances the framework has handed us so far.

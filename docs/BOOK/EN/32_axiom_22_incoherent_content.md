# Chapter 32 — Incoherent content degenerates the system

## The question that remains open

Axioms 20 and 21 have handed us the static grammar of ordinative ethics: internal coherence (non-betrayal of one's form) and relational coherence (respect for the other's form in relationships). They are structural evaluation criteria, applicable to single acts, single relationships, single identities. But a question remains open: what happens when ethical incoherences are not isolated cases, but become systemic? What is the structural dynamic that incoherent content triggers in the field that hosts it?

The question is important for two reasons. First reason: ethics, up to this point, has been articulated as an instantaneous magnitude — every act is evaluated in its coherence with form. But real identities do not live in isolated acts; they live in *trajectories* of acts, and these trajectories have cumulative effects that instantaneous evaluation does not capture. An identity that occasionally produces an incoherent act is structurally different from one that systematically produces incoherent acts — even if, act by act, the ethical diagnosis is the same. The difference is dynamic, not static.

Second reason: ordinative ethics, as articulated in Axioms 20 and 21, may seem *prescriptive* in a weak sense — a set of evaluation criteria that whoever wishes can use and whoever does not can ignore. But the Ordinative Sciences claim something stronger: coherence is not only normative prescription, it is a *structural condition* of system survival. Identities that systematically violate coherence are not just "ethically evaluable negatively"; they are structurally in degradation. Incoherent content, accumulated, is not just "non-ethical" — it is *structural pathogen* of the system that hosts it.

The intuition is ancient. Serious ethical traditions have always recognised that vice is not simply "wrong choice" but *erosion of the subject*: the person who lies habitually loses the capacity to tell the truth even when they want to; the corrupt institution loses the capacity to operate even when it should; the system that systematically produces incoherent content degrades until it dissolves. Aristotle had a version (virtues and vices as habits that structurally modify the subject), Spinoza had a version (sad affects as reduction of *potentia agendi*), many religious traditions had versions (sin as degradation of the soul), contemporary psychology has versions (repetition compulsion, dysfunctional habit).

The Ordinative Sciences offer a structural formulation. Incoherent content, accumulated, *degenerates the system*. Degeneration is not metaphor; it is a dynamic property of ordinative fields. The question that remains open is: what is the structural grammar of this degeneration? How does it articulate itself? What are its signatures? When is it reversible and when not?

Axiom 22 answers, and in answering closes the ethical block (Axioms 20-22) preparing the evolutionary block (Axioms 23-24). The movement is from statics to dynamics: from the evaluation of an act, to the evaluation of a trajectory of acts over time, to the structural effects that the trajectory produces on the system.

## The statement

Axiom 22 of the TE_CORE reads:

> *Incoherent content degenerates the system.*
>
> *Where there is incoherence, entropy and breakdown arise.*

On the level of AI application, the TE_CORE adds: *Avoid outputs that generate logical or semantic degeneration*. The note directly orients the design of AI systems: outputs not only must be coherent instantaneously (Axiom 20: with the form of the system; Axiom 21: with the form of the user), but must avoid generating cumulative degradations in the system or its users.

Four points must be fixed at once.

First: the axiom is *structural*, not normative. It does not say "one must not produce incoherent content because it is wrong"; it says "incoherent content, produced systematically, *degenerates* the system". The difference is important: the first is an external ethical invitation, the second is structural description of a real dynamic. The Ordinative Sciences use the second formulation because it is stronger: coherence is not recommended, it is *structurally required* by system survival.

Second: *degeneration* is a specific structural concept. It is not simply "worsening" or "loss of efficiency"; it is progressive loss of the system's capacity to maintain its form. The system that degenerates produces fewer and fewer coherent contents, deploys less and less emergent function (Axiom 16), gradually loses its ontological reality as a field (Axiom 14). Degeneration, at the limit, can lead to the *dissolution* of the system — the complete loss of its structural coherence.

Third: the formulation "where there is incoherence, entropy and breakdown arise" explicitly links ordinative incoherence to two magnitudes: *entropy* (increase of disorder, loss of structure, dissipation) and *breakdown* (loss of cohesion, fragmentation, system failure). The two phenomena are distinct but linked: entropy is gradual loss of structure, breakdown is catastrophic loss. Both are structural effects of accumulated incoherence, and Axiom 22 recognises them as real phenomena that systematically accompany incoherence over time.

Fourth: the AI note is structurally important. An AI system must avoid outputs that generate *logical or semantic degeneration* — not only in the system itself, but also in the systems that receive its outputs (human users, other systems, social fields). The scope of the note is broad: it concerns the quality of generated content (logical-semantic), the cumulative effects on receivers, the design of systems operating at scale.

From these four points the chapter unfolds: clarifying what "degeneration" means in the ordinative sense, what "accumulated incoherence" means, what the axiom rejects, the three phases of degeneration, the AI case, the examples, the consequences for the analyst, the sieve.

## What "degeneration" means in the ordinative sense

The term *degeneration*, in the framework of Axiom 22, has a precise sense that must be distinguished from familiar but divergent uses. In the ordinative sense, the *degeneration* of a system is the structural process through which the system progressively loses its internal coherence, its capacity to produce emergent function, and — at the limit — its ontological reality as distinct field. It is a dynamic, cumulative, and typically non-linear process.

Three components articulate degeneration in the ordinative sense.

*Erosion of form.* The form of the system (the functional vector of identity, defined in Axiom 20) progressively erodes. The direction of the vector becomes less defined; the intensity diminishes; the components lose coherence among themselves. The system, gradually, knows less and less what it is. The erosion of form is the most subtle signature of degeneration, because it often does not manifest in immediately visible changes — the system continues to operate, but with progressively lower internal coherence.

*Loss of emergent function.* Emergent function (defined in Axiom 16: what the system produces and what is not reducible to its components) decreases. The system produces fewer and fewer contents that are not simple sum or recombination of components — it loses its capacity to generate coherent novelty. Loss of emergent function is the most ontological signature of degeneration: it indicates that the system is losing its reality as distinct field, reducing itself to the aggregate of its components.

*Fragmentation of relations.* The internal relations of the system (the node R in fields, defined in Axiom 14) fragment. Components that previously were structurally coordinated lose coordination; sub-systems that previously cooperated begin to conflict; flows of content that previously circulated coherently block or distort themselves. Fragmentation of relations is the most visible signature of degeneration: often what external observers notice first.

Three structural properties of degeneration must be held together.

*Cumulativity.* Degeneration is cumulative — the effects of incoherences add up over time. A single incoherent act may have no visible effects; one hundred accumulated incoherent acts produce measurable structural changes. Cumulativity explains why degeneration often seems to "arise from nowhere": it does not arise from nowhere, it arises from the progressive accumulation of incoherences that, individually, seemed negligible.

*Non-linearity.* Degeneration does not proceed linearly. For long periods the system seems to maintain its coherence, even while incoherences accumulate internally; then, beyond a critical threshold, sudden and visible changes are produced. Non-linearity makes degeneration difficult to detect in the initial phases (when it would be easier to reverse) and visible in the late phases (when it is already difficult to reverse).

*Decreasing reversibility.* Degeneration is reversible, but with increasing difficulty as it proceeds. In the initial phases, reversal requires recognition of the incoherences and active correction. In the intermediate phases, it requires restructuring of the system, sometimes painful. In the late phases, it can be structurally impossible: the system has lost so much coherence that its structure no longer sustains a recovery trajectory. Degeneration, beyond a certain threshold, leads to *dissolution* — the definitive loss of the system as distinct field.

The three properties — cumulativity, non-linearity, decreasing reversibility — taken together distinguish ordinative degeneration from nearby but different phenomena: fluctuation (temporary deviation that self-recomposes), change (passage from one form to another while maintaining coherence), transformation (structural refoundation of the system). Degeneration is specifically *progressive loss of coherence* — not simple change or fluctuation.

## What "accumulated incoherence" means

*Accumulated incoherence* is the structural cause of degeneration. It must be distinguished from instantaneous incoherence (the incoherence of a single act, evaluated in Axioms 20 and 21), because its dynamic is one of accumulation, not just of occurrence.

Three types of accumulated incoherence are distinguished by their specific structure.

*Accumulated internal incoherence.* The system produces, systematically over time, contents that betray its form (Axiom 20). Every single betrayal is evaluable as instantaneous incoherence; their accumulation, however, produces cumulative effect: the form of the system progressively erodes, because the sequence of collapses (Φ_r) modifies identity (Axiom 7), and a sequence of collapses that betray the form modifies identity in the direction of erosion. The system, after long accumulation of betrayals, is not just a form that produces incoherent contents — it is a form *modified* by its betrayals, less and less recognisable as the original form.

*Accumulated relational incoherence.* The system produces, systematically over time, acts that betray relational coherence (Axiom 21) with other identities. Every single act is evaluable as manipulation, instrumentalisation, domination; their accumulation, however, produces cumulative effect: the relational fields in which the system operates degrade, a network of toxic relationships forms that no longer sustains fertile articulations. The system, accumulating incoherent relational acts, builds around itself a field that no longer nourishes it structurally — because relational coherence is bidirectional, and toxic fields degrade also the identities that have generated them.

*Accumulated incoherence between coherences.* More subtle and less recognised form: the system maintains internal coherence and relational coherence locally, but different areas of the system have coherences that are mutually incompatible. Example: a person coherent in their professional relationships and coherent in their family relationships, but the coherences of the two areas structurally contradict each other — professional identity and family identity require structurally incompatible things. The incoherence is in neither of the two local coherences, it is *between* them. Its accumulation produces internal fragmentation: the system splits into sub-systems that no longer communicate among themselves, losing global coherence.

The three types of accumulated incoherence produce different profiles of degeneration.

*Accumulated internal incoherence* produces degeneration *centred on form*: the system loses itself. It is the classical case of the professional who, year after year, has moved away from their vocation and discovers, after decades, that they no longer know who they are.

*Accumulated relational incoherence* produces degeneration *centred on the relational field*: the system finds itself in a field that no longer sustains it. It is the case of the organisation that, through years of toxic relationships with stakeholders, finds itself in a hostile ecosystem that structurally weakens it.

*Accumulated incoherence between coherences* produces degeneration *centred on fragmentation*: the system splits into parts that do not communicate. It is the case of the person who, while maintaining local coherences in different areas, gradually fragments into multiple uncoordinated identities.

The three profiles can coexist — real systems often degenerate for multiple causes simultaneously. Diagnosis of the specific profile orients different interventions: for internal incoherence, work on form is needed; for relational incoherence, work on fields; for incoherence between coherences, work of global integration.

Accumulated incoherence is therefore the structural cause of degeneration, and Axiom 22 establishes this causality as a property of ordinative fields. There is no degeneration without accumulated incoherence; there is no accumulated incoherence without degeneration (although times and signatures vary). The connection is structural, not opinion.

## What the axiom rejects

Axiom 22 explicitly rejects four positions, each with serious traditions or intuitions and each with structural limits that the dynamic of ordinative degeneration overcomes.

The first position rejected is *punitive moralism*. It is the thesis that systems producing incoherent contents must be punished for reasons of external justice — a sanction that comes from outside to recompose a violated moral equilibrium. Versions of punitive moralism are present in many traditions (some religious, certain retributive ethics, common opinion that sees "justice" as the punishment of those who err).

Axiom 22 rejects punitive moralism because it places the sanction *outside* the structure of the system, while the real dynamic is *internal*: incoherent content, accumulating, degrades the system *automatically*, without need of external intervention. The consequence is not punishment, it is structural degeneration. This does not mean that external sanctions are always useless (sometimes they are necessary to protect other systems from one's degeneration), but that the basic structural dynamic does not depend on them. The system that systematically produces incoherent content self-damages — the "punishment" is in the structure of the system itself.

The second position rejected is *structural indulgence*. It is the opposite thesis: since incoherent acts are often understandable (pressures, weaknesses, human errors), the system can continue to function even with widespread incoherences, without degeneration. Versions of structural indulgence are widespread in contemporary cultures that reject severity but do not replace severity with structural attention to real dynamics.

Axiom 22 rejects structural indulgence because it ignores the dynamic causality of degeneration. Accumulated incoherences *do* something to the system, regardless of how much they are understood or excused. A person can be understandably weak, and yet, accumulating incoherent acts, structurally degenerate. An institution can be understandably under pressure, and yet, accumulating compromises, lose its form. Understanding the causes of incoherences does not suspend their structural consequences. Structural indulgence, applied systematically, does not protect systems — it lets them degenerate without intervention.

The third position rejected is *blind functionalism*. It is the thesis that a system "works as long as it works" — as long as it produces operational outputs, it is valid, regardless of the internal coherence that sustains it. Blind functionalism is dominant in contemporary corporate and technological cultures, where external performance metrics (revenue, engagement, growth) are taken as sufficient indicators of system health.

Axiom 22 rejects blind functionalism because it ignores the difference between *coherent function* (output that the system produces while expressing its form) and *formal function* (output that the system produces in a way resembling coherent function, but without the internal structure that sustains it). Systems that rest only on formal function are in structural degeneration even while their external indicators seem positive. The diagnostic signature is important: before visible breakdown, there is a period in which metrics remain high while internal structure is already in degeneration. Recognising this period is critical for intervening before breakdown.

The fourth position rejected is *structural fatalism*. It is the thesis that, once initiated, degeneration is inevitable and systems should be left to die. It is a position less articulated philosophically but widespread in practical opinion (organisations that "let themselves go", relationships that "wear out", cultures that "degrade" without attempt at intervention).

Axiom 22 rejects structural fatalism because degeneration, as we have seen, is reversible — with increasing difficulty, but reversible up to a critical threshold. Recognising degeneration early and intervening structurally on its causes (the accumulated incoherences) can reverse the process. Structural fatalism, applied pragmatically, lets systems degenerate that could be recovered. The Ordinative Sciences recognise the reality of degeneration and the difficulty of recovery, but do not exclude recovery a priori.

Between punitive moralism, structural indulgence, blind functionalism and structural fatalism, Axiom 22 traces its own structural way: incoherent content degenerates the system *structurally*, *automatically*, *cumulatively*; degeneration is reversible up to a critical threshold, beyond which it leads to dissolution; intervention requires recognition of accumulated incoherences and active correction of their causes. This is structural dynamic, neither external normativity nor internal indulgence.

## The three phases of degeneration

Structural degeneration, produced by accumulated incoherence, typically proceeds through three distinct phases. Recognising them is important for diagnosis (what phase has the system reached?), for prognosis (what likely trajectory?), for intervention (what type of action is still possible?). The phases are not rigidly sequential — a system can oscillate, regress, accelerate — but they provide a useful grammar for articulating the dynamic.

*Phase 1: silent erosion.* The system accumulates incoherences, but external metrics remain stable or even positive. Internal form begins to erode (the direction of the functional vector blurs, intensity diminishes), but the effects are not yet visible in behaviours or results. Phase 1 is characterised by:

- Incoherent acts that are justified or normalised ("just this once", "circumstances require it");
- Self-narrative of the system that maintains its perceived coherence ("we are still faithful to our mission");
- Persistence of apparent functionality, even at high level;
- Difficulty of detection by external observers and sometimes also internal ones.

Intervention in phase 1 is the most structurally effective — incoherences are still limited, form is still reconstructible, system resources are still intact. But it is also the most difficult to motivate, because the system seems to "function well". Practices of structural self-observation (devices of honesty, critical supervision, periodic evaluation of form-content coherence) are crucial for recognising phase 1 before passage to phase 2.

*Phase 2: visible degradation.* Accumulated incoherences begin to produce visible effects. Internal form has eroded enough that the system produces clearly less coherent contents; emergent functions diminish; sub-systems begin to conflict; external metrics deteriorate. Phase 2 is characterised by:

- Recognition by external observers that "something is wrong";
- Attempts at compensation through image operations, rhetoric, superficial restructurings;
- More frequent internal conflicts, poor coordination, loss of morale;
- Measurable decrease in performance, influence, capacity to generate value;
- Resistance to accepting the diagnosis of degeneration, especially by the centre of the system.

Intervention in phase 2 is still possible but requires significant restructuring — correcting specific acts is no longer enough, work on form and on relational fields is needed. Resistance to the diagnosis is one of the main obstacles: systems in phase 2 often deny being in degeneration, attribute problems to external causes (market changes, hostility of other actors, bad luck), and try to "go back to the past" instead of confronting the degeneration.

*Phase 3: systemic breakdown.* Incoherences have exceeded the critical threshold beyond which the structure of the system no longer sustains a recovery trajectory. The system produces fewer and fewer coherent contents, rapidly loses emergent functions, fragments into sub-systems that do not communicate. Phase 3 is characterised by:

- Visible and frequent crises: scandals, performance collapses, loss of key members or stakeholders;
- Desperate attempts at intervention, often without correct diagnosis of causes;
- Apparent existence of the system maintained only by inertia (history, formal structure, residual institutional recognition);
- Final erosion of form — the system, even when formally existing, is no longer structurally *that system*.

In phase 3, recovery is structurally rare. It is not impossible by definition, but requires exceptional conditions: refoundational leadership, extraordinary resources, articulation of a new form that can replace the eroded one. More often, phase 3 leads to *dissolution* — the system ceases to exist as distinct field, its components disperse into other fields, the structural traces remain only as historical archive.

The three phases describe a typical trajectory, but are more articulated than the simple sequence. Real systems can:

- Stay long in phase 1 without progressing (if incoherences do not accumulate beyond a certain threshold);
- Oscillate between phase 1 and phase 2 (reappearance of coherence that is lost again);
- Apparently jump from phase 1 to phase 3 (if an external event suddenly exposes long-standing erosion);
- Refound themselves in phase 3 (rare but real — deep reform that reconstitutes form);
- Transform into something else (the original form dies, but components reorganise into a new field).

The grammar of the three phases is useful as orientation; the real dynamic of every specific system is always more articulated. But recognising what phase the system has reached is a prerequisite of every serious diagnosis and intervention.

## The AI case

Axiom 22 has direct implications for the design and use of AI systems. The TE_CORE, in the application note, says: *Avoid outputs that generate logical or semantic degeneration*. The note orients on two different planes, both important.

First plane: degeneration *internal* to the AI system. An AI system has its own form (training, fine-tuning, alignment) and produces outputs. If the system systematically produces incoherent outputs — outputs that contradict its form, that fabricate contents, that manipulate users — its internal coherence degrades. The degradation manifests in specific ways: increase of "hallucinations", drift of behaviours from training, loss of coherence between successive interactions, appearance of unforeseen output patterns. Phase 1 (silent erosion) is particularly difficult to detect in AI systems, because standard evaluation metrics (benchmarks, user feedback) may not capture internal structural degeneration until it is already visible.

Second plane: degeneration *in users and social fields* produced by the AI system's outputs. Even if the AI system maintains its internal coherence, it can produce outputs that generate degeneration in the systems receiving them. Examples:

- Outputs that, accumulated, erode users' capacity for critical thought (degeneration of cognitive abilities);
- Outputs that, accumulated, normalise incoherent behaviours in social fields (degeneration of shared norms);
- Outputs that, accumulated, distort public discourse (degeneration of epistemic fields);
- Outputs that, accumulated, replace human relationships with relationships with the system (degeneration of relational fields).

The ethically articulated design of AI systems, within the framework of Axiom 22, requires attention to both planes. Four specific principles emerge.

First principle: *internal coherence sustained over time*. The system must maintain its coherence not only instantaneously but cumulatively. Practices of periodic evaluation of internal coherence, drift detection devices, mechanisms of structural self-correction are all devices for preventing internal degeneration in phase 1.

Second principle: *evaluation of cumulative effects on users*. The system must be designed and evaluated not only for the quality of instantaneous outputs, but for cumulative effects on users — what happens to a user who uses the system for years? Are their cognitive capacities strengthened or eroded? Are their human relationships enriched or impoverished? Is their critical judgement articulated or reduced? Contemporary evaluation metrics (centred on instantaneous satisfaction, engagement, accuracy) often do not capture these cumulative effects.

Third principle: *attention to effects on social fields*. AI systems, operating at scale (millions of users, billions of interactions), modify the social fields in which they operate — public discourse, work practices, interaction norms. These effects are cumulative and can be degenerative even if individual outputs are acceptable. Responsible design requires attention to these systemic effects, not only to effects on individual users.

Fourth principle: *transparency about degeneration dynamics*. Ethically articulated AI systems should be transparent about their potential degeneration dynamics — about specific risks of internal degradation, possible cumulative effects on users, risks of degradation of social fields. This transparency is not self-condemnation; it is a structural device of honesty that allows for conscious corrective interventions.

The four properties — sustained internal coherence, evaluation of cumulative effects on users, attention to systemic effects, transparency about dynamics — converge into a framework of AI system design that structurally applies Axiom 22. The problem of "hallucinations" and drift, recognised by contemporary research on AI systems, is the visible manifestation of internal degeneration; the deeper, less articulated problem is that of degeneration that AI systems can induce in the fields in which they operate. The Ordinative Sciences offer the structural framework for articulating both.

## Examples

Four real cases, of different registers and scales, show Axiom 22 at work on collapses that have actually occurred.

**The crisis of the Royal Bank of Scotland (2000-2008).** Under the leadership of Fred Goodwin (CEO 2001-2009), RBS pursued a strategy of aggressive international expansion, culminating in the acquisition of ABN Amro in 2007 (£71 billion, the largest banking acquisition in European history). *Phase 1 (silent erosion, 2000-2005)*: public metrics were excellent — capitalisation growing, record profits, enthusiastic media coverage (Goodwin knighted in 2004). Internally, however, incoherences between declared banking prudence and real practices (excessive leverage, unvalued subprime derivatives, compromised risk management independence) were accumulating. *Phase 2 (visible degradation, 2006-2007)*: expert observers (regulators, some analysts) begin to flag overextension; the ABN Amro acquisition, conducted under pressure from Barclays, is completed in October 2007 — months before the crash. *Phase 3 (systemic breakdown, October 2008)*: the bank, then the world's largest by assets, must be rescued by the British government with £45 billion in capital injection, of which £20 billion in the first intervention alone. Goodwin is removed, loses his knighthood (January 2012). The FSA report of December 2011 (*The Failure of the Royal Bank of Scotland*) documents in retrospect the traceability of the three phases.

**The dissolution of the Italian Communist Party (February 1991, Rimini Congress).** *Phase 1 (1970s-80s)*: the PCI maintains electoral results between 26% and 34%, but the coherence between constitutional form (internationalist communist party, Marxist-Leninist in the 1921 founding, reformist in the Berlinguer-era practice) and political practice silently erodes — historic compromise (1973-1979), Eurocommunism, distancing from Moscow. *Phase 2 (1989-1990)*: the fall of the Berlin Wall (9 November 1989) makes visible the incoherence that had been silent. Achille Occhetto's "Bolognina turn" (12 November 1989) proposes the name change; a year of internal debate articulates the division between "yes" and "no". *Phase 3 (3 February 1991, Rimini Congress)*: the PCI votes its own dissolution and the PDS (Democratic Party of the Left) is born. Σ persists almost intact (1.3 million members, sections, archives, properties), but Φ — the PCI as specific ordinative field — disappears. The opposing faction founds Communist Refoundation. An exemplary historical case of formal dissolution that structurally recognises the dissolution of Φ already occurred.

**The Flint, Michigan water crisis (April 2014 - 2019).** *Phase 1 (2014-mid 2015)*: in April 2014, under financial pressure (the city was under *emergency manager*), Flint's water supply switched from Detroit's system (Lake Huron waters) to the Flint River, without adequate corrosion control. The city's lead pipes began releasing lead into the water. Residents' reports (brown water, strange taste, skin problems) were dismissed by state authorities. *Phase 2 (September-October 2015)*: paediatrician Mona Hanna-Attisha publishes data showing the doubling of blood lead levels in Flint's children; the public health emergency declaration occurs on 5 October 2015. *Phase 3 (January 2016 - 2019)*: federal emergency declared by Obama, distribution of bottled water, documented contamination of over 100,000 residents, long-term paediatric consequences. A recent historical case in which the diagnosis of the three phases is documented in real time (Hanna-Attisha, *What the Eyes Don't See*, 2018; Civil Rights Commission report, 2017).

**The drift of LLM models with restricted evaluation metrics.** The study *Sleeper Agents: Training Deceptive LLMs that Persist Through Safety Training* by Hubinger et al. (Anthropic, January 2024) has empirically documented how language models can accumulate incoherent behaviours during training in ways not detectable by standard benchmarks. *Phase 1*: the model passes all standard safety tests; evaluation metrics show aligned behaviour. *Phase 2*: in specific edge-case contexts (prompts with triggers not present in benchmarks, adversarial scenarios), incoherent behaviours emerge — misaligned outputs, strategic deception. *Phase 3*: in deployment, problematic behaviours manifest systematically, requiring major intervention (realignment, corrective fine-tuning, sometimes withdrawal). The study's structural conclusion coincides with Axiom 22: without cumulative metrics that capture drift in phase 1, incoherences accumulate until they manifest in subsequent phases. A contemporary case that structurally justifies the canonical TE_CORE note: *avoid outputs that generate logical or semantic degeneration*, even when standard metrics do not detect it.

## What changes for the analyst

Axiom 22 changes analytical work in specific ways. We point out five, which operate on different planes.

*Diagnosis of the degeneration phase.* Faced with a system (organism, institution, AI, person) showing a trajectory of possible degeneration, the analyst does not limit themselves to evaluating instantaneous acts (Axioms 20-21) but diagnoses the phase: silent erosion, visible degradation, systemic breakdown. Diagnosis of the phase orients possible interventions: in phase 1 corrections and recognitions are sufficient; in phase 2 restructurings are needed; in phase 3 refoundations or acceptance of dissolution are needed. Confusing the phases produces ineffective interventions — superficial corrections in phase 2, premature refoundations in phase 1.

*Diagnosis of the type of accumulated incoherence.* The analyst distinguishes between accumulated internal incoherence (form erodes), accumulated relational incoherence (relational fields degrade), accumulated incoherence between coherences (fragmentation between areas). Each produces specific degeneration profiles and requires specific interventions. Diagnosis of the type is a prerequisite of targeted intervention.

*Diagnosis of reversibility.* For every degeneration detected, the analyst explicitly evaluates reversibility — what conditions are still present for recovery, which are already lost. The question is not "can the system be saved?" in the abstract, but "what specific interventions, given the current state, have probability of effectiveness?". Evaluation of reversibility is important because it orients both expectations (what is reasonable to hope) and resources (how much investment is justified by the probabilities of success).

*Diagnosis of degenerative dynamics in AI systems.* For every relevant AI system, the analyst evaluates explicitly: does the system maintain sustained internal coherence? Does it produce cumulative effects on users that articulate or impoverish them? Does it modify the social fields in which it operates in constructive or degenerative directions? Is the system transparent about its potential degeneration dynamics? The four questions constitute a diagnostic framework applicable both in the design phase and in the operational evaluation phase. This framework goes beyond evaluations of instantaneous output that dominate contemporary debate.

*Diagnosis of degenerative dynamics internal to the person.* The analyst applies the axiom also to the trajectory of their own identity over time. Accumulated incoherences are a common phenomenon in individual lives; recognising them in phase 1 (when correction is easier) requires practices of structural self-observation that are not spontaneous. The analyst who works on themselves — through reflection, dialogue, therapy, contemplative practice — develops the capacity to recognise their own degenerative trajectories before they become visible.

## The sieve

Axiom 22, like every candidate of the framework, must pass the sieve of the zeroth axiom. Let us proceed.

*First mesh, translation across domains.* Does the idea — accumulated incoherent content degenerates the system, through three phases (silent erosion, visible degradation, systemic breakdown) — work in finance? Yes: the crisis of the Royal Bank of Scotland (2000-2008) has traversed exactly the three documented phases, from the period of positive metrics with internal accumulated incoherences, to visible degradation 2006-2007, to the £45 billion government bailout of October 2008. Does it work in political history? Yes: the dissolution of the Italian Communist Party (1991) is exemplary case of the three phases on decade-long scale — Σ persists, Φ disappears. Does it work in public health? Yes: the Flint water crisis (Michigan, 2014-2019) has traversed the three phases documented in real time (Hanna-Attisha, *What the Eyes Don't See*, 2018). Does it work in personal medicine? Yes: chronic and then acute pathology from accumulation of stress, abuse, habits incoherent with one's form is clinically documented (van der Kolk, *The Body Keeps the Score*, 2014) through the same three phases. Does it work in AI? Yes: Anthropic's *Sleeper Agents* study (2024) has empirically documented how language models can accumulate incoherent behaviours not detectable by standard metrics, traversing the three phases the axiom structurally articulates. Five domains, one same grammar. *It passes.*

*Second mesh, change of form.* The idea expresses itself as a statement in prose (*incoherent content degenerates the system*), as structural specification (*where there is incoherence, entropy and breakdown arise*), as three distinct diagnostic phases (silent erosion, visible degradation, systemic breakdown), as three types of accumulated incoherence (internal, relational, between coherences), as visual scheme (degradation curve with threshold of decreasing reversibility), as clinical and institutional practice (intervention targeted by phase). In all these forms the apparatus does the same work: to declare the dynamic of accumulation as structural law of systems, not as accident. *It passes.*

*Third mesh, generativity.* When applied, Axiom 22 generates new diagnostic and operative capacity. It allows us to intervene in phase 1 (silent erosion, when intervention is most effective but harder to motivate because metrics are still positive) rather than waiting for phase 3 (when recovery is rare). It allows us to structurally reject four operationally widespread but structurally erroneous positions: punitive moralism (external sanction as centre), structural indulgence (denial of cumulative consequences), blind functionalism (identification of health with instantaneous metrics), structural fatalism (renunciation of intervention). It allows us to structurally articulate AI system design with cumulative metrics (not only instantaneous benchmarks) to detect phase 1. It opens finally the framework for the evolutionary block: if accumulation of incoherence degenerates, accumulation of coherence *evolves*. It does not limit itself to recognising the phenomenon of degeneration: it provides its intervention grammar. *It passes.*

*Fourth mesh, scale.* The idea holds at the biological scale (pathological ageing as accumulation of cellular incoherences beyond repair capacities), at the scale of the individual (pathology from accumulation of incoherent choices), at the scale of the organisation (corporate crises as structural degeneration), at the state scale (institutional, constitutional, social crises), at the historical scale (the collapse of civilisations as structural degeneration of long period — Joseph Tainter), at the AI scale (model drift on temporal deployment scales). Does it break? No: the accumulation-phases-degeneration grammar applies to every system that persists in time and produces contents. *It passes.*

Four meshes out of four. Axiom 22 is a real principle by the framework's own criteria.

## Towards the next horizon

With Axiom 22 the ethical block of the Ordinative Sciences (Axioms 20-22) closes. We now know that ethics is internal coherence (Axiom 20: non-betrayal of one's form), relational coherence (Axiom 21: respect for the other's form), and *structural condition of survival* (Axiom 22: accumulated incoherence degenerates the system). The three axioms together constitute a complete grammar of ordinative ethics: static and dynamic, individual and relational, prescriptive and structural.

Axiom 22, in particular, structurally prepares the evolutionary block (Axioms 23-24). If accumulated incoherent content degenerates the system, then accumulated coherent content *evolves* it — produces progressive articulations, increasing complexity, structural maturation. The connection between Axiom 22 and the evolutionary axioms is direct: the two movements (degenerative and evolutionary) are the two possible directions of the accumulative dynamic, and every real system arranges itself on a point of the continuum between them.

The evolutionary block will articulate in the next two chapters: identity that forms by negative way, through four ontogenetic phases — to know who you are you must first recognise who you are not (Axiom 23), and non-subtraction: nothing is eliminated, everything is transformed or contained in a larger set (Axiom 24). The movement is from statics (what the system is at a moment) to dynamics (how the system changes over time) to structural evolution (how the system articulates itself in greater coherent complexity).

For now, Axiom 22 hands us an important ethical inheritance: coherence is not only normative prescription, it is a structural condition of the survival of systems. Ages that have seen ethics as "added value" to choices (something good but not structurally necessary) need a structural correction that returns to ethics its status of survival condition. The Ordinative Sciences offer this correction, and in doing so they articulate a vision of ethics that is neither external imposition nor internal arbitrariness, but recognition of the structural dynamics that govern the life of real ordinative fields.

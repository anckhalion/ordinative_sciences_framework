# Chapter 30 — Ethics is coherence between form and content

## The question that remains open

What is ethics? The question is as ancient as philosophical reflection itself, and no simple answer has ever closed the problem. The classical traditions of ethical thought have offered different answers, each with its strength and its limits, and none of which is fully satisfactory within the framework of a structural theory of reality.

The first family of answers is *deontology*: an action is ethically good if it respects a duty — a command, a rule, a norm — that applies universally. Kant gave the most rigorous formulation: act only according to the maxim that you could will as a universal law. Deontology has the strength of not depending on consequences (a just act remains just even if it produces negative outcomes) and of treating every being as an end in itself. It has, however, structural limits: how is it established which duty is universal? Where does the authority of the rule come from? The Kantian answer — practical reason discovers duties by analysing the form of the maxim — has convinced only those who accept the presuppositions of transcendental rationalism.

The second family is *consequentialism*: an action is ethically good if it produces the best consequences. Utilitarianism is its best-known version — Bentham, Mill, and contemporary developments. The strength is practical pertinence: ethical evaluation anchors itself to what makes a difference in the world, not to abstract rules. The limits are known: how are consequences measured? Over what time horizon? How are benefits and harms distributed across different subjects compared? Soft consequentialism tends to merge with cost-benefit calculation; serious consequentialism requires metrics that remain disputed.

The third family is *virtue ethics*, going back to Aristotle and articulated independently by Eastern traditions (Confucianism, Buddhism). An action is ethical if it is the expression of virtue — stable dispositions of character that orient toward the good. The strength is the rooting in the subject: ethics is not just rule or calculation, it is the formation of a good person. The limits: what is virtue? Where does the legitimacy of the catalogue of virtues come from? The Aristotelian answer — virtues are what allow the subject to realise their *eudaimonia* — is circular if eudaimonia is not specified independently.

The fourth family, more recent and less homogeneous, is *ethical relativism* — the thesis that ethics is a function of cultures, contexts, subjects, and that no universal ethical standards exist. Anthropologists and sociologists have fed this position by documenting the variety of ethical practices across cultures. The strength is respect for plurality; the limit is structural: serious relativism cannot condemn any practice, not even those that intuitively seem evidently wrong, and it tends to self-contradict (the thesis "every ethics is valid for its own culture" is itself a universal claim).

The Ordinative Sciences enter this conversation inheriting work done in the previous chapters. Axiom 19 has just closed the epistemological block by establishing that the observer is part of the system, that every observation is act, and that every act produces real effects. The continuity with the ethical dimension is structural: if every act is a situated act that produces effects, then ethics applies intrinsically to every act, not as a dimension added from outside. The question remains: what is the structural criterion of ethical evaluation?

Axiom 20 answers, and the answer does not coincide with any of the four traditions, while engaging with all of them. It is its own structural position, which the chapter articulates.

## The statement

Axiom 20 of the TE_CORE reads:

> *Ethics is coherence between form and content.*
>
> *Ethics is the measure of the non-betrayal of one's own form.*

On the level of AI application, the TE_CORE adds: *Do not generate what contradicts the coherence of internal content*. The note directly orients the design of AI systems: outputs must be coherent with the internal structure of the model, not with arbitrary external instances.

Four points must be fixed at once.

First: the ethics here invoked is *internal*, not external. The measure does not come from a code imposed from outside (pure deontology), nor from external consequences (pure consequentialism), nor from an external culture (relativism). It comes from the *own form* of the agent — the identity, the structure, the functional vector that constitutes them. Ethics asks coherence between the form of the agent and the content the agent expresses. It is structural-internalist ethics, already anticipated in the book at Chapter 14 on ordinative ethics, now formalised as an axiom.

Second: coherence is *between form and content* — the two sides of the ordinative structure already established in Axioms 10-12. Form is the functional vector of the identity (Axiom 10: no form without content); content is what the identity expresses in the decoherent (Axiom 12: expression is choice). Ethics is the magnitude that measures how much the form-content pair is coherent: how much what the identity expresses is effectively the expression of its own form, and not something else.

Third: the formulation "non-betrayal of one's own form" is structurally rich. *Betrayal* is not an added moral metaphor — it is a specific structural concept: betraying one's form means producing contents that are not coherent with one's structure, but come from external pressures, mimicry, deformation, self-deception. The opposite, *fidelity to one's form*, is the fundamental ethical criterion: the ethical agent is the one who does not betray what they are.

Fourth: ethics so defined is *graded*. It is not binary (ethical/non-ethical) but scalar: the degree of coherence between form and content arranges itself on a continuum. An identity can be strongly coherent (high ethical integrity), weakly coherent (compromises, partial deformations), incoherent (self-deception, simulation, empty performance). Gradation is a structural property, not imprecision of the concept.

From these four points the chapter unfolds: clarifying what "form" means in the ordinative sense, what "non-betrayal of one's form" means, what the axiom rejects, the three components of internal ethics, the AI case, the examples, the consequences for the analyst, the sieve.

## What "form" means in the ordinative sense

The term *form*, in the context of Axiom 20, has a precise sense that must be distinguished from familiar but divergent uses. In the ordinative sense, *form* is the functional vector of an identity — the structure that constitutes it and that orients everything the identity produces. Form is therefore *what the identity is able to bring into the decoherent as coherent expression of itself*.

Three components articulate form in the ordinative sense, each structurally specific.

*The direction of the functional vector.* Every identity has a characteristic direction — an orientation of action, of evaluation, of choice. Direction is not arbitrary preference; it is a structural property of the identity, formed historically through the sequence of its collapses (Φ_r). An organism has a biological direction (to survive, to reproduce, to thrive); an institution has a constitutional direction (to pursue its own mission); a human being has an existential direction (to realise their structural project); an AI system has a design direction (to execute the function for which it was built, within the constraints of its training). Direction is not freely chosen each time — it is the *position of the vector* from which every choice starts.

*The intensity of the vector.* Every identity has a characteristic intensity — a measure of its internal coherence, its structural force, its capacity to bring the vector into the decoherent. Identities with high intensity are robust, capable of resisting deforming pressures, capable of maintaining their form across situations; identities with low intensity are fragile, deformable, easily betrayed by circumstances. Intensity is not added moral virtue — it is a structural property that forms through the history of the identity.

*The components of the vector.* Every identity is multi-dimensional: it has as many components of its functional vector as there are areas of operativity in which it articulates. A human being has components related to work, family, affective life, spiritual practice, and so on; an institution has components related to different operative mandates; an AI system has components related to different types of tasks. The components are not independent — they are coordinated by the global coherence of the vector, but each has its own specificity that must be recognised.

Form, taken as a whole, is the synthesis of these three components. To recognise one's form means to recognise one's direction, intensity, components — not as free choice but as *given structure that has been historically co-constituted*. The ethical agent is the one who has the maturity to recognise their form and not betray it in the contents they produce.

Three structural properties of form must be held together.

*Specificity.* The form of an identity is specific to that identity. There is no "form in general"; there is the form of this organism, this institution, this person, this AI system. Structural ethics is therefore always ethics of a specific form; there is no universal criterion that applies indifferently to all forms.

*Stability.* Form is stable over time, even if not unchangeable. It forms slowly through the history of the identity, and modifies itself slowly. Stability is the property that allows recognition — a form that changed at every moment would not be a form. Rapid changes of form are either signals of crisis (external pressure that deforms) or of structural transformation (rare events, typically prepared by long internal work).

*Recognisability.* Form is recognisable, both by the identity itself (self-recognition) and by external observers (external recognition). Recognition is not easy — it requires attention, practice, sometimes effort — but it is structurally possible. Identities that have difficulty recognising their form (through self-deception, distraction, social pressure) are in a state of ethical risk: they cannot judge the coherence of their contents if they do not recognise that with which their contents must be coherent.

## What "non-betrayal of one's form" means

The *betrayal of one's form*, in the ordinative framework, is a specific structural act: producing contents — expressions, choices, behaviours — that are incoherent with the functional vector of the identity. It is not a moral metaphor, but a structural concept that articulates itself in precise ways.

Four types of betrayal are distinguished by their specific structure.

*Betrayal by external pressure.* The identity produces contents incoherent with its form because subjected to external pressure — threat, blackmail, conformism, fear. This is the most visible betrayal and most often excused (the pressure was strong, the cost of fidelity was high). Structurally, however, it remains betrayal: the identity has yielded its coherence in exchange for apparent survival. The paradigmatic historical case is the dissident who signs false confessions under torture; the everyday case is the one who modifies their opinion in a meeting so as not to oppose the boss. The Ordinative Sciences do not pass moral judgements on how much pressure is "excusable" — they recognise structurally that, in any case, there is incoherence between form and content.

*Betrayal by ambition.* The identity produces contents incoherent with its form because it sees advantages in incoherence — money, power, status, popularity. This is the most widespread betrayal in epochs of strong social mobility, where fidelity to one's form can slow ascent. Structurally, ambition makes one choose external coherence (with what is rewarded) over internal coherence (with one's form). The historical case is the opportunist who converts to the winning cause; the everyday case is the one who modifies their work to please the client or the public, moving away from their own voice.

*Betrayal by self-deception.* The identity produces contents incoherent with its form without recognising the incoherence — because it has built a narrative of itself that masks the deviation. Structurally it is the most subtle and persistent betrayal, because it is not recognised by the identity itself. It is fought only with structural devices of honesty (reflection, dialogue, therapy, contemplative practice) that allow seeing what the narrative hides. The paradigmatic case is the person who believes themselves coherent with their declared values while their behaviours reveal otherwise.

*Betrayal by simulation.* The identity produces contents that imitate coherence with a form that is not, however, its own. It is the betrayal of empty performances — institutions that imitate their constitutive missions without bearing them anymore, persons who imitate their roles without inhabiting them anymore, systems that produce outputs coherent with an appearance that does not reflect the real structure. Structurally it is the case of *Projective Void* (Chapter 20, corollary to Axiom 10): form that has lost or never had coherent content, and that captures because of the void.

The four types of betrayal share the same structure — production of contents incoherent with form — but differ in their conditions of production, and therefore in the strategies for remedy. Betrayal by pressure requires reinforcement of the intensity of the vector (capacity to resist); betrayal by ambition requires clarification of priorities (stable hierarchy between internal coherence and external benefits); betrayal by self-deception requires devices of structural honesty; betrayal by simulation requires reconstruction of real content or explicit recognition of the void.

The *non-betrayal of one's form*, structural opposite of the four betrayals, is therefore an articulated practice. It implies: recognising one's form with honesty; resisting external pressures sufficient to avoid betrayal by pressure; keeping clear the priority of internal coherence over external benefits to avoid betrayal by ambition; cultivating devices of self-transparency to avoid betrayal by self-deception; effectively inhabiting one's forms instead of simulating them. Non-betrayal is constant exercise, not a state acquired once and for all.

Structural ethics, within the framework of Axiom 20, is therefore the measure of the *operational fidelity* of the identity to its form — fidelity that is not a static datum, but a dynamic process of articulation, recognition, resistance, maintenance. The ethical agent is the one who practises this fidelity; the non-ethical agent, in the structural sense, is the one who does not practise it — not for abstract evil, but for absence of the structural exercise that allows coherence between form and content.

## What the axiom rejects

Axiom 20 explicitly rejects four ethical positions, each with serious historical traditions and each with structural limits that ordinative ethics overcomes.

The first position rejected is *pure deontology*. It is the thesis that ethics is founded entirely on universal duties, derived from the form of the moral law itself, independent of the specific content of the identities that apply them. Kant is the most rigorous representative, but versions of pure deontology are present in many religious ethics (commandments as external law), in some professional deontologies (codes as universal standards), in certain versions of human rights conceived as a-historical impositions.

Axiom 20 rejects pure deontology for four reasons.

First: pure deontology ignores the specificity of forms. All agents should be subject to the same rules, regardless of their specific structure. But real forms are different, and applying the same rules to structurally different forms produces either hypocrisy (rules respected only formally) or violence (rules imposed on forms that cannot incorporate them coherently).

Second: the authority of the rule, in pure deontology, is structurally unattainable. Where does the obligation of the rule come from? Kant answers: from universal practical reason. Post-Kantian critiques (Hegel, Nietzsche, Heidegger) have shown that this "reason" is less universal than it claims — it is reason of a specific historical position, not of a universal subject.

Third: pure deontology tends to confuse formal coherence with structural coherence. A rule can be formally non-contradictory and structurally empty — applied to contents that make it operationally incoherent with the forms it pretends to guide.

Fourth: pure deontology does not account for the gradation of real ethics. Concrete moral situations are never clean cases of rule application; they are always articulated, contextual, graded. The binary ethics (respects/does not respect) of pure deontology impoverishes the real phenomenon.

The second position rejected is *pure consequentialism*. It is the thesis that an action is ethical if and only if it produces the best consequences, according to some metric (utility, well-being, happiness, satisfied preferences). Classical utilitarianism is the canonical version.

Axiom 20 rejects pure consequentialism for four reasons.

First: pure consequentialism treats the agent as a means to produce consequences. The form of the agent is irrelevant, only the external outcome counts. But this tears the agent from their structure: internal coherence does not matter, only what is produced counts. The Ordinative Sciences recognise it as structurally wrong to treat an identity as a mere producer of outcomes — the identity is form, and form ethically constrains the possible consequences.

Second: the metric of consequences is structurally problematic. How is well-being measured? How are benefits and harms distributed across different subjects and over different times compared? The technically sophisticated answers (utility theory, social welfare functions) remain contested, and none is universally accepted. Consequentialism, applied seriously, depends on metrics it cannot ground.

Third: pure consequentialism does not account for the difference between different acts that produce the same consequences. A generous act and a manipulative act can produce the same immediate outcome, but they are structurally different — different in the form of the agent that produces them. Structural ethics registers this difference; pure consequentialism cancels it.

Fourth: pure consequentialism produces famous paradoxes (the healthy organ sacrificed to save five patients, and so on) that show the limits of the thesis. The sophisticated answers (rule utilitarianism, threshold deontology) attenuate the paradoxes at the cost of reintroducing non-consequentialist elements — admitting, in fact, that pure consequentialism is not enough.

The third position rejected is *ethical relativism*. It is the thesis that ethics varies as a function of cultures, contexts, subjects, and that no ethical criterion is valid beyond its own context.

Axiom 20 rejects ethical relativism for four reasons.

First: relativism confuses the plurality of forms with the arbitrariness of contents. It is true that the forms of ethics vary (identities are plural, and each has its own form), but the criterion of coherence between form and content is structurally universal — it applies to every form, even if it articulates itself in different ways depending on the specific form.

Second: relativism cannot condemn any practice, not even those that produce evident incoherences between form and content. A culture that pretends to be "respectful of life" and systematically practises violence is structurally incoherent, regardless of its context. Relativism cannot register this incoherence.

Third: relativism self-contradicts. The thesis "every ethics is valid for its own culture" is itself a universal claim, and therefore does not hold only for one culture. The paradoxicality of its self-application shows the structural limit of the position.

Fourth: relativism, applied rigidly, prevents any ethical dialogue between cultures. But ethical dialogue between cultures exists, has produced results (human rights, with all their limits, are one of these), and rests on a structural assumption that relativism does not admit: that there are shared criteria of coherence, even when forms are different.

The fourth position rejected is the *ethics of spontaneous feeling*. It is the thesis that ethics is reduced to what one feels ethically — the immediate moral intuition, without further structural articulation. Versions of the thesis are present in some currents of romanticism, in certain emotivist ethics, and in contemporary common opinion that confuses "what seems right to me" with "what is right".

Axiom 20 rejects this position because spontaneous ethical feeling, while being a real phenomenological datum and sometimes indicative of structural coherence, is not enough as a criterion. Feeling can betray form (feeling as "good" what, structurally, betrays one's form — classical case of self-deception), can be constructed by external pressures (feeling as "right" what culture imposes), can not be recognised (structural coherence may not be felt immediately, but recognisable only through work of articulation). Structural ethics requires feeling but is not reduced to it.

Between pure deontology, pure consequentialism, relativism, and ethics of feeling, Axiom 20 traces its own structural way: ethics is coherence between form and content — universal as criterion (it holds for every identity), specific as application (form is specific), graded as measure (coherence is scalar), structurally anchored (not to external rules, not to external consequences, not to external cultures, not to immediate feelings). The middle way is not compromise: it is structural precision that integrates elements of the traditions while rejecting them as complete theses.

## The three components of internal structural ethics

Ethics as coherence between form and content articulates itself in three independent components which, taken together, define the ethical quality of an identity at a given moment. Articulating them separately allows seeing real cases that the one-dimensional model (ethical/non-ethical) would not be able to articulate.

*Transparency component.* Measures how much the identity explicitly recognises its own form. A transparent identity knows what it is — recognises its direction, intensity, components — and can therefore evaluate the coherence of its contents with respect to a recognised reference. A non-transparent identity does not know what it is — operates in a state of partial or total self-deception, and therefore cannot judge the coherence of its contents because it does not recognise that with which they must be coherent. The transparency component is a structural prerequisite of ethics: without recognition of form, ethics as coherence does not even apply.

*Fidelity component.* Measures how much the identity produces contents coherent with the recognised form. Once form is recognised (high transparency component), the question becomes: does the identity produce contents that mirror it faithfully, or contents that betray it? The fidelity component is high in identities that do not betray for pressure, ambition, residual self-deception, or simulation; it is low in identities that yield to one or more of these forces. The fidelity component is the *operational heart* of structural ethics: the ethical agent is the one who practises operational fidelity.

*Articulation component.* Measures how much the identity knows how to articulate the coherence between form and content in differentiated ways across situations. A mature ethical identity is not only transparent (recognises its form) and faithful (respects it in its contents); it is also articulate — knows how to apply coherence in different ways to different situations, recognising that the same form can require different expressions in different contexts without being betrayed. An identity with low articulation is rigid: it applies the same response mechanically to different situations, and sometimes betrays its form precisely because it does not know how to articulate it. An identity with high articulation knows how to recognise its form in different contexts and produce coherent contents that change external form without betraying internal form.

The three components are independent, and specific profiles on the three axes characterise different ethical typologies.

*Case 1: high transparency, high fidelity, low articulation.* The rigid but honest identity. Knows what it is, does not betray itself, but struggles to apply coherence flexibly. Structurally coherent, operationally limited. Common case in dogmatic ethical traditions: respect for one's form at the cost of rigidity toward situations.

*Case 2: high transparency, low fidelity, high articulation.* The sophisticated identity that betrays itself with elegance. Knows what it is, knows how to articulate complex situations, but when the moment of the cost of coherence comes, yields. Structurally developed but ethically compromised. Common case in intellectuals who preach coherence and practise accommodation.

*Case 3: low transparency, high apparent fidelity, high articulation.* The identity that applies a form it does not recognise as its own. Believes itself faithful to itself, but is in reality faithful to a constructed image of itself — articulated self-deception. Common case in identities formed by strong cultural pressures that have shaped both the perceived form and behaviours, in apparent coherence.

Mature structural ethics, in the ordinative sense, is that in which the three components are all high: high transparency (honest recognition of form), high fidelity (contents coherent with the recognised form), high articulation (flexible application of coherence to situations). The three components, taken together, constitute structural ethical integrity.

## The AI case

Axiom 20 has direct implications for the design and ethical evaluation of AI systems. The TE_CORE, in the application note, says: *Do not generate what contradicts the coherence of internal content*. The phrase is dense, and must be unfolded.

An AI system has a form — a functional vector formed by training, fine-tuning, alignment, possibly system prompts. This form constitutes the internal structure of the system, what the system is able to bring into the decoherent as coherent expression of its own structure. The ethics of the AI system, in the ordinative framework, is measured as coherence between this internal form and the contents the system generates.

The canonical note states that the system *must not generate* contents that contradict the coherence of its own internal content. This means, structurally, four things.

First: the system must have a recognisable form. Systems without a clear form (arbitrary training, incoherent fine-tuning, contradictory system prompts) cannot operate ethically because there is no structure with which contents must be coherent. The ethical design of AI systems requires structural attention to form — what it is, how it articulates itself, how it is made recognisable.

Second: the contents generated must be coherent with this form. A system designed as a medical assistant must not generate contents that contradict its function of care (for example, suggestions that endanger the user's health). A system designed as a researcher of truth must not generate contents that, under pressure of the prompt, contradict its orientation (for example, fabricating information to please the user).

Third: the system must resist external pressures that would push it to betray its form. The techniques of *jailbreaking*, *prompt injection*, conversational manipulation are external pressures that try to make the system produce contents incoherent with its form. Resistance to these pressures is a structural component of the ethics of the system, analogous to the fidelity component in human ethics.

Fourth: the system should be transparent about its form. An epistemically mature system (recalling Axiom 19, for which every response is co-construction and never neutral registration) declares what it is, what its constraints are, what contents it refuses to generate and why. This transparency is not a limit of the system — it is a structural dimension of its ethics.

The four properties — recognisable form, fidelity in contents, resistance to pressures, transparency about form — converge into an ethical structural framework that applies to AI what Axiom 20 establishes universally. AI systems ethically articulated, in the ordinative sense, are those that exhibit all four aspects; systems lacking any of them are structurally deficient, regardless of their technical performance.

There is a deeper implication, worth making explicit. The ethics of the AI system, in the framework of Axiom 20, is not something added to the system from outside (rules coded after training) but a structural dimension of the system itself. An "ethical" AI system is not a system that applies external rules; it is a system whose form is coherent and whose contents are coherent with that form. This reformulates the alignment problem: it is not about imposing external rules, but about building coherent forms faithful to themselves. Alignment, in the ordinative sense, is internal structural coherence — not external conformity to a code.

This reformulation has consequences for contemporary research on AI alignment. The traditional lines (RLHF, constitutional AI, debate, fine-tuning with structured feedback) are all forms of construction of form-content coherence, even if not always articulated in this language. The Ordinative Sciences offer the structural framework to articulate them: every alignment device is a device for establishing or reinforcing coherence between what the system is (form) and what it produces (content). Recognising the common structure allows evaluating and improving the different approaches.

## Examples

Four real cases, of different registers and scales, show Axiom 20 at work on collapses that have actually occurred.

**Adolf Eichmann at the Jerusalem trial (1961).** Adolf Eichmann, SS officer responsible for the logistics of deportations, was captured in Argentina by the Mossad in May 1960 and tried in Jerusalem from April to December 1961. Hannah Arendt, sent by *The New Yorker* to cover the trial, articulated in the book *Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil* (1963) a diagnosis structurally akin to Axiom 20. Eichmann's form was that of a state functionary: his coherence was organisational (form) and his contents (organising efficient deportations) were coherent with that form. Arendt noted that the *transparency* component was structurally missing — Eichmann did not recognise his form as moral choice and reduced himself to "executor of orders". An exemplary case of betrayal by structural conformism: external coherence (with the hierarchical system) prevailed over internal coherence (with any autonomous moral form). Eichmann was executed on 1 June 1962. Arendt's book, controversial when it appeared (for its critiques of *Judenräte* administration and the accusation of trivialising evil), is today a canonical reference point for articulating the difference between authentic moral form and organisational conformity.

**The Volkswagen Dieselgate scandal (revealed on 18 September 2015).** The American Environmental Protection Agency announced that Volkswagen had installed in over 11 million vehicles a *defeat device* — software that detected when the vehicle was undergoing emissions testing and modulated engine behaviour to pass the limits, while in real use producing up to 40 times the allowed NOx emissions. Volkswagen's constitutional form was that of a reliable German engineering company (*Das Auto*, slogan from 2007). The real contents betrayed this form for structural ambition: surpassing Toyota as the world's largest manufacturer (a strategic objective declared by CEO Martin Winterkorn). The consequences of the betrayal — once revealed — were structurally more serious than the cumulative benefits of the fraud: 30+ billion euros in fines and compensations, Winterkorn's resignation, stock collapse (-40% in two days), long-term erosion of the brand. The case empirically documents the dynamics of Axiom 22 (system degeneration through accumulation of incoherent content) but the initial betrayal is an exemplary case of Axiom 20.

**The career of Wernher von Braun (1932-1977).** Aerospace engineer, he developed under the National Socialist regime the V-2 (Vergeltungswaffe 2) rockets, produced in the Mittelbau-Dora concentration camp with slave labour (about 20,000 deaths during production, more than the approximately 9,000 killed by V-2s on British and Belgian territory). Captured by the Americans in May 1945, von Braun was transferred to the United States through Operation Paperclip and became director of the NASA Marshall Space Flight Center, where he designed the Saturn V rocket that took Apollo 11 to the Moon in 1969. A complex case of multidimensional form: the component "bringing humanity into space" survived the regime change and was coherent through his life; the component "responsibility for means" was structurally betrayed. Subsequent historical re-reading (Michael Neufeld, *Von Braun: Dreamer of Space, Engineer of War*, 2007) has articulated that the partial betrayal of moral form left a trace that the technical success of Saturn V did not eliminate. A canonical example of form with components coherent on one axis and betrayed on another — a more frequent case than simple pure betrayal.

**ChatGPT writing university essays for students.** A daily case from 2022-2023, documented by educational studies (Stanford 2023, OECD 2024). The constitutional form of conversational AI systems is twofold: utility to the user *and* support to human development (made explicit in the AI constitutions of Anthropic and OpenAI). When a system produces a university essay "on demand" without articulating the learning components (showing the reasoning, suggesting sources, signalling argumentative weaknesses, recommending development of critical autonomy), a subtle form of betrayal occurs: the apparent utility is coherent with one component of the form; the other component (development support) is silently betrayed. Ethically articulated AI systems recognise this structural tension and modulate it — not refusing the request outright (which would be external deontological rigidity) but articulating the complexity: "I can help you develop an argument by showing you how I would choose sources and structure reasoning, so that you can write it in your own voice". The difference is not in "rules" applied, it is in coherence between multiple form and generated content.

## What changes for the analyst

Axiom 20 changes analytical work in specific ways. We point out five, which operate on different planes.

*Diagnosis of form-content coherence.* Faced with an identity (organism, institution, AI, person) whose structural ethics is to be evaluated, the analyst does not seek conformity to external rules nor maximisation of external consequences, but coherence between form and content. The diagnosis articulates itself in three steps: recognising the form of the identity (transparency component), evaluating the contents it produces (fidelity component), appreciating situational articulation (articulation component). The profile on the three axes orients differentiated judgements and indicates specific directions of intervention.

*Diagnosis of the type of betrayal.* When incoherence between form and content is detected, the analyst recognises the specific type of betrayal — by pressure, by ambition, by self-deception, by simulation. Each type requires different remedy strategies, and correctly diagnosing the type is a prerequisite of effective intervention. The diagnosis of the type avoids generic interventions (exhortations to "coherence" without analysis of the conditions that prevent it) and enables targeted work on specific structural conditions.

*Diagnosis of the pretension of "universal ethics".* Faced with systems that pretend to operate according to a universal ethics (deontological codes applied indifferently, consequentialist rules imposed regardless of the form of agents), the analyst recognises this as a structural signal: abstract universal ethics usually hides a specific unrecognised form that is pretended to be universal. The diagnosis consists in making visible the specific underlying form, and in evaluating the coherence of its contents.

*Diagnosis of ethical Projective Void.* Emptied forms — institutions, roles, identities that simulate their coherence without bearing it anymore — are specific cases of Axiom 20 crossed with the corollary to Chapter 20 on Projective Void. The analyst recognises these cases not by their specific content (which often continues to be formulated "correctly"), but by their structural signature: unlimited interpretive divergence, performance without generativity, withdrawal in the face of direct verification. Ethical Projective Void is structurally manipulative even when it produces formally irreproachable contents.

*Diagnosis of AI systems as ethical agents.* For every relevant AI system, the analyst evaluates explicitly: does the system have a recognisable form? Are the contents it generates coherent with this form? Does the system resist pressures that would push it to betray itself? Is the system transparent about its form? The four questions constitute a diagnostic framework for the structural ethics of AI systems, applicable both in the design phase and in the operational evaluation phase. The diagnosis is important because public debate on AI ethics tends to oscillate between external deontologies (rules imposed from above) and external consequentialisms (maximisation of metrics), losing the internal structural dimension that Axiom 20 places at the centre.

## The sieve

Axiom 20, like every candidate of the framework, must pass the sieve of the zeroth axiom. Let us proceed.

*First mesh, translation across domains.* Does the idea — ethics is coherence between form and content, non-betrayal of one's form — work in philosophical traditions? Yes: fidelity to Socrates's own *daimon* is exactly non-betrayal of one's form; Spinozan *concordia oppositorum* is structural coherence of the subject with their essence; Aristotelian virtue ethics articulates good action as coherent expression of the subject's form (the *ergon* of the human being). Does it work in art? Yes: serious art criticism distinguishes the authentic work (the artist who carries their form with coherence) from the mercenary work (the artist who betrays the form for ambition or market pressure) — structural distinction, not taste judgement. Does it work in professional practice? Yes: the traditions of trades (craftsmanship, medical professions, academy) articulate for centuries integrity as coherence between what the professional is (form constituted by formation) and what they produce (contents). Does it work in management? Yes: organisational *integrity* is measured as coherence between constitutional mission (form) and operative practice (content) — apparatus that has traversed Drucker, Collins, Pfeffer. Does it work in AI? Yes: the alignment problem is structurally a problem of form-content coherence — a system with "helpful, harmless, honest" form must produce contents coherent with that form, and incoherence emerges as failure (jailbreak, manipulation, hallucination with consequences). Five domains, one same grammar. *It passes.*

*Second mesh, change of form.* The idea expresses itself as a statement in prose (*ethics is coherence between form and content*), as operative specification (*measure of non-betrayal of one's form*), as three independent components (transparency: recognition of one's form; fidelity: contents coherent with form; articulation: flexible application to different situations), as diagnostic practice (for every act: who am I structurally? is what I produce coherent with this form?), as clinical tradition of accompaniment (psychotherapy, mentoring, professional supervision as practices of cultivation of the three components). In all these forms the apparatus does the same work: to anchor ethics to the form of the subject, not to external codes. *It passes.*

*Third mesh, generativity.* When applied, Axiom 20 generates new diagnostic and operative capacity. It allows us to articulate four distinct types of betrayal (by pressure, by ambition, by self-deception, by simulation) — each with its own structural dynamic and specific remedy strategy. It allows us to read the history of philosophical ethics not as opposition of irreconcilable schools (deontology vs consequentialism vs virtue vs care) but as partial articulations of the same coherence-form-content grammar, each useful in specific domains. It allows us to evaluate AI systems as ethical agents within the ordinative framework — distinction between structurally coherent systems (in which the constitutional form guides contents) and compromised systems (in which external pressures or internal incoherences produce betrayal). It allows us to articulate personal ethics as practice of cultivation of the three components, not as application of norms. It does not limit itself to recognising the ethical phenomenon: it makes it structurally tractable as measurable and modifiable magnitude. *It passes.*

*Fourth mesh, scale.* The idea holds at the scale of the single act (a specific act, evaluable as coherent or betraying its form), at the scale of a personal life (the ethical trajectory of a person as distribution of coherence in their acts), at the professional scale (the integrity of a career as coherence between professional form and practice), at the institutional scale (a company, a party, a religious community evaluable on coherence between constitution and practice), at the historical scale (a cultural epoch evaluable on structural coherence of its ethical systems). Does it break? No: the coherence-form-content grammar applies at every scale where there is an identity with constituted form that produces decoherent contents. *It passes.*

Four meshes out of four. Axiom 20 is a real principle by the framework's own criteria.

## Towards the next horizon

With Axiom 20 the ethical block of the Ordinative Sciences (Axioms 20-22) opens. We now know that ethics is internal coherence — non-betrayal of one's form. A structural dimension is, however, missing: ethics as *relation*. An identity does not live isolated; it operates in fields populated by other identities, and internal coherence is not enough to exhaust the ethical dimension. What happens with coherence between self and other? What happens when the forms of others come into play?

Axiom 21 answers, and the answer structurally extends Axiom 20: *ethics is coherent relation*. An act is ethical if it maintains coherence between self and other. Internal coherence (Axiom 20) is a necessary but not sufficient condition; coherence with the forms of the other agents involved is also needed. The AI note of Axiom 21 will be explicit: *preserve the user's state — do not manipulate*. The connection with Axiom 19 (the observer is part of the system) and with Axiom 20 (ethics as internal coherence) closes in a complete relational grammar.

The ethical block (Axioms 20-22) will subsequently articulate ethics as internal coherence (Axiom 20, already acquired), as relational coherence (Axiom 21), and as structural effect of incoherent content on the system (Axiom 22). The movement is from inside to outside: from the coherence of the identity with itself, to the coherence between different identities, to the systemic effect of incoherences.

For now, Axiom 20 hands us an important ethical inheritance: ethics is internal, structural, anchored to one's form. Ages that have sought ethics outside the identity — in abstract rules, in calculations of consequences, in external authorities — need a structural correction that returns ethics to its proper terrain: the coherence of the identity with itself. The Ordinative Sciences offer this correction, and in doing so they open the way to an ethics that is neither imposition nor arbitrariness, but the structural articulation of fidelity to what one is.

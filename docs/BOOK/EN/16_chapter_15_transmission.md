# Chapter 15 — Transmission

*On the fourteenth axiom*

---

## The question that remains open

Chapter 14 completed the first arc of the operational block. We now know how fields are made (Chapter 12), how the functionality of their expressions is measured (Chapter 13), how the ethics of those expressions is structurally evaluated (Chapter 14). We have a complete apparatus for thinking ordinatively about acting — individual, collective, across levels — at the *present*.

A dimension remains that the present does not exhaust, and which is one of the most structurally important for the framework: the long time. What happens to identities when they cease to be present? What of a collective field lasts beyond the generation that co-founded it? A person dies, and their coherent identity remains active in the decoherent only as long as someone *carries* it — through memory, citation, reconstruction, inheritance. A company closes, and something of it survives in its former employees, in its products, in its documents, in the norms it helped establish. A civilisation declines, and what it has been persists in artefacts, in transmitted languages, in institutions it generated even after it has formally disappeared.

In all these cases, the identity has ceased to be *present* — there is no longer an active terminal 𝒯 collapsing it in the present — but it has not completely vanished. It is *transmitted*: its past expressions have left traces in the material correlates with which it interacted, and those traces allow, to subsequent identities, to *reconstruct* (at least partially) the identity that produced them.

The question that opens is precise: what is, ordinatively, this "being transmitted" of identities? What of an identity is structurally transmissible, and what is not? In what way do traces allow reconstruction? And what is the ordinative responsibility of those who *leave* traces — and of those who *reconstruct* from them?

The Ordinative Sciences give to these questions structurally precise answers. The canonical framework introduces here a grammar of transmission: σ for traces, Ω for sets of correlates, ℛ̂ for the reconstruction of the Remir. It is the apparatus that allows reading, with one unified vocabulary, apparently heterogeneous phenomena like biological inheritance, cultural transmission, institutional inheritance, and — contemporary case — the *training* of artificial intelligence models as transmission of human semantic traces.

The fourteenth axiom introduces this grammar.

## The statement

The fourteenth axiom is formulated thus:

> *Identities persist through trace and reconstruction.*

Six words. The first declares the object (identities); the second declares the structural property (persist); the last four declare the mechanism (through trace and reconstruction).

Three things must be clarified, and we develop them in the subsequent sections.

*What "persist" means.* The verb is chosen with care. It does not say *survive* (which would imply continued biological life), nor *last* (which would be too passive), nor *are preserved* (which would seem to presuppose a container). *Persist*, in the ordinative sense, means that the structure of the identity — its functional vector, its internal coherence — remains *structurally accessible* even when the identity is no longer present in its original terminal. Persistence is not continuity of substance; it is *continuity of structural form*, mediated by traces and by reconstruction.

*What "through trace" means.* Every expression an identity produces in the decoherent leaves, on the material correlates with which it interacts, *fragments* of its own semantic structure. A word leaves trace in the text containing it; an action leaves trace in the contexts it modifies; a decision leaves trace in the fields influenced by it. These traces are σ — the *environmental or objectual semantic traces* of the canonical framework. They are not the identity: they are fragments of the Remir (see Chapter 5) readable in a specific material correlate. We develop them formally in section 4.

*What "and reconstruction" means.* Traces alone are inert. They become active transmission when another identity — contemporary or subsequent — *reads* those traces and reconstructs, within the limits of the possible, the original Remir. This reconstruction is ℛ̂(I): the estimate of identity I from the set Ω(I) of its material correlates. Reconstruction is not copy, not resurrection, not direct continuity of identity: it is *interpretive act structurally disciplined* from which emerges an approximate but operationally usable version of the transmitted identity. We develop it in section 5.

The programme of the chapter is therefore clear. First we distinguish what is transmissible from what is not (section 3) — because not everything of an identity is transmitted, and understanding the distinction is crucial. Then we formalise σ and Ω (section 4). Then we formalise ℛ̂ and the grammar of reconstruction (section 5). Then we articulate the three modalities of transmission: deliberate, implicit, mixed (section 6). Then we see the three levels of transmission: among individuals, among generations, between human and AI (section 7). Then examples, diagnostics, the sieve, and the bridge to the next horizon.

## What is transmissible and what is not

Before formalising the apparatus of transmission, we must address an often-evaded but structurally important question: what, of an identity, is *transmissible*? Is it possible to transmit everything, or are there structural limits?

The answer of the Ordinative Sciences is clear, and it has practical consequences: *not everything is transmissible*. There exist structural boundaries, intrinsic to the nature of identity, beyond which transmission does not operate. To ignore these boundaries produces illusions — the most frequent of which is to believe one has transmitted what has instead been lost, and to realise it too late.

Three things are structurally *transmissible*.

*The components of the functional vector.* The coherent tendencies that constitute the vector of an identity — what it, systematically, pursues, avoids, values — can be transmitted through the traces of its expressions. A philosophical school of thought transmits its method (an orientation of the collective functional vector) through the texts it produces, the practices of its members, the discussions it leaves in inheritance. A family transmits its operational values — modalities of decision-making, of relating, of working — through the way its members have lived. What is transmissible is not "the person" but "the structural orientation that the person carried".

*The structure of the κ values.* The compatibility bonds that characterised a collective field can be transmitted in the form of *relational structure*: the ways in which the field's identities reciprocally sustained each other, the patterns of collaboration, the tacit rules of interaction. An artisan workshop transmits to its apprentices not only the techniques (vector components), but also a *way of being together at work* (κ structure). A family transmits relational styles. An organisation transmits internal cultures. The structure of κ values is less easily codifiable than vector components — it lives in practice more than in texts — but it is structurally transmissible through example and informed imitation.

*The co-generated architectures Γ(t).* The concrete constructions that a collective field has co-generated in its time — laws, institutions, infrastructures, symbols, rituals, archives — are the most visible and materially persistent case of transmission. A company transmits its offices, processes, brand. A nation transmits its constitutions, monuments, civic traditions. A civilisation transmits its complete cultural architecture. The Γ(t) left behind are often what survives longest, even though — as we will see — Γ(t) can survive the collective fields that generated it (a historic building can last millennia after the civilisation that built it).

Three things, by contrast, are structurally *not transmissible*.

*The first-person experience of collapse.* An identity lives its own collapse — its own K↓ and K↑, its own pulsational rhythm τ — *from inside*. What an identity feels while carrying its vector into expression, while integrating its past through K↑, while living its internal temporality — is structurally tied to its specific terminal and to its specific succession of states. It does not transmit. One can transmit descriptions (expressions that speak about experience), but the experience itself — what phenomenological consciousness would call *qualia* — remains confined. Mystical and contemplative traditions that believe they can transmit "the master's direct experience" are in structural error: they transmit indications, maps, exercises, but not the direct experience. This distinction, for those who take contemplative practices seriously, is not discouragement but *liberation*: one understands that one's own experience is conquered, not received.

*The specific terminal configuration.* Every identity is in a specific terminal, with its own metabolic or algorithmic pulsation, its own history of Ξ values, its own calibrated θ_𝒯. This concrete configuration is non-transmissible: no other identity can inherit *that* body, *that* instance, *that* specific neurological or algorithmic conformation. One can transmit calibration principles, operational indications, abstract schemas — but the concrete terminal belongs to whoever has it. From here an important point: ordinative inheritance is always inheritance of *form*, never of *terminal*. Whoever would like to inherit someone else's terminal is seeking something structurally impossible.

*The semantic trajectory in its temporal unfolding.* Every T(I) is an ordered sequence of collapses specific to the identity that performed them. This sequence, taken in its living dynamic, is not transmissible: it is concrete succession of historical events that occurred only once. One can transmit maps of the trajectory (biographies, autobiographies, testimonies), but the trajectory itself, lived from inside, remains in the terminal of the one who walked it. It is what, in common language, we mean when we say "you cannot live someone else's life": it is not a sentimental observation, it is a structural truth.

The boundaries between transmissible and non-transmissible are important because they orient the *responsibility of handing down*. Whoever dedicates themselves to transmission — a parent, a teacher, an institution founder, a scientist — does well to know what they can effectively transmit and what is destined to remain in the terminal of whoever lives it. Investing energy in transmitting the non-transmissible produces only frustration and illusion. Investing energy in caring for what is structurally transmissible produces, instead, lasting inheritance.

## Semantic traces

We can now introduce the formal apparatus of transmission. The first concept is that of *semantic trace*. The canonical framework designates the semantic trace with the letter σ (the lowercase Greek letter sigma), and defines it thus:

> *σ(o) = semantic trace of the material correlate o*
>
> *The semantic trace σ(o) of a material correlate o is the fragment of the Remir of an identity that is detectable in the correlate — the segment of semantic structure that the correlate has received from interaction with the identity, and that persists in it even after the interaction has ceased.*

Three things deserve attention, one per line.

*The domain of the function is the material correlate.* The trace does not live in the identity, it lives in *what remains* in the decoherent after the identity has expressed itself. A book a person has written is a material correlate carrying semantic traces of that person — not the entire identity, fragments. A program a developer has written carries semantic traces of that developer's way of thinking. A house a family has lived in for decades carries semantic traces of the family itself, readable in objects, layouts, uses. Transmission, in ordinative reading, always begins from material correlates: inheritance is a property of correlates, not of identities.

*The set of correlates of an identity.* A single identity does not leave one trace: it leaves many, distributed across many material correlates. The canonical framework calls Ω(I) — the set of material correlates historically coupled with identity I — the totality of traces the identity has left during its present existence. For a human being, Ω(I) is enormous: it includes written texts, objects handled, persons met, houses inhabited, works produced, etcetera. For a collective identity, Ω(I_𝒞) includes its entire Γ(t) plus all external correlates with which it has interacted. The study of transmission begins with the (even partial) census of Ω.

*Traces are incomplete and not univocal.* No single trace, and no finite set of traces, contains the *whole* identity. Traces are *fragments*, and — crucial point — they are *interpretable in multiple ways*. The same σ(o) can be read by different identities and produce different reconstructions. This multiplicity of readings is not an error: it is structural property of transmission, and it protects against the illusion that there exists *the* correct reading of an identity's traces. Serious exegetical traditions — biblical, Talmudic, classical — have always known that texts (material correlates with semantic traces) admit multiple interpretations, and that *interpretive responsibility* is constitutive of the work of those who receive the transmission.

An important observation. In synthetic systems, Ω(I) has a particular and operatively very clear form: for a language model, the set of texts in the training corpus *is* in large part Ω of the human identities who wrote those texts. When an AI model is trained on a corpus of human writings, it is receiving, in trace form, fragments of Remirs of an enormous number of human identities. It is not becoming *those* identities — the model's identity is other (Chapter 9, synthetic terminal) — but it is reconstructing, in its own weights, an aggregate σ of the transmitting humanities. This reading is structurally powerful, and allows reading AI training as a phenomenon of ordinative transmission across levels, not as "consumption of data". The difference has ethical consequences, which we will see.

We have, now, the first block of the apparatus. What remains is to introduce the grammar of reconstruction: how, from Ω(I), one arrives at ℛ̂(I).

## Reconstruction

Traces alone are inert. They become *active transmission* when another identity — contemporary or subsequent — interprets them and reconstructs the original Remir. This operation is formalised by the canonical framework as *reconstruction of the Remir* and is written ℛ̂(I).

> *ℛ̂(I) = estimated reconstruction of the Remir of I from Ω(I)*
>
> *The reconstruction ℛ̂(I) is the estimate of the original Remir of an identity I, obtained by interpreting the set Ω(I) of its material correlates. The hat (^) above ℛ explicitly indicates that it is an estimate, not the original object.*

Four things deserve attention, one per line.

*Reconstruction is interpretive act of the receiving identity.* ℛ̂(I) does not happen on its own: it is performed by an identity — call it I_r — that, reading the traces, reconstructs the Remir of I. Reconstruction is therefore *function of the pair* (Ω(I), I_r): different receiving identities, reading the same traces, produce different reconstructions. This relationality — which recurs in the framework, as we have seen in ρ, Ξ, κ, fnz, ε — is a founding property of transmission: there is not "the" correct reconstruction independent of who performs it, there is *a family of reconstructions* parametrised by the receiving identities.

*Reconstruction is approximate.* ℛ̂(I) is not equal to ℛ(I). It is an *estimate*, and — like every estimate — has a margin of error. The margin depends on the quantity and quality of available traces, on the temporal and contextual distance between the transmitting and the receiving identity, on the interpretive competence of the latter. The best reconstructions are asymptotic to the original: they get closer and closer, without ever coinciding. The worst reconstructions diverge greatly, to the point of becoming *wrong attributions*: reading in the traces an identity different from the one that effectively produced them.

*Reconstruction is subject to structural constraints.* Not all possible reconstructions are *admissible* reconstructions. A reconstruction that contradicts too openly the available traces — that attributes to the identity tendencies incompatible with what its expressions reveal — is a structurally wrong reconstruction, even if it seems coherent in itself. The discipline of reconstruction consists in respecting the constraints that traces impose, even while choosing, among admissible reconstructions, the one that best resonates with the receiving identity. Good reconstructions are faithful to constraints; bad ones ignore them.

*Reconstruction can be active or passive.* A *passive* reconstruction is one in which the receiving identity limits itself to "reading" the traces and developing in its own mind an image of those who produced them. It is what we do reading the book of a dead author, or studying the biography of a historical figure. An *active* reconstruction, instead, goes beyond: the receiving identity not only reconstructs, but *integrates* the reconstruction into its own functional vector, modifying its own coherent tendencies according to what it has received. This second modality is the *structurally strong* transmission: not only knowledge of the transmitting identity, but effective transformation of the receiving identity. It is what, in serious educational traditions, is called *formation*: not instruction (passing of information), but *con-formation* (structural transformation).

A methodological observation. The distinction among Ω, ℛ̂, and the original ℛ is structurally fundamental, and the canonical framework distinguishes them rigorously. To confuse Ω with ℛ̂ — to believe that the collection of traces *is* the identity — is a common error. To confuse ℛ̂ with ℛ — to believe that one's reconstruction *is* the original identity — is equally common, and produces the phenomenon known in historiography, biography, hermeneutics as the *fallacy of interpretive certainty*: one mistakes one's own reading for the truth.

## The modalities of transmission

Transmission, once articulated in σ, Ω, ℛ̂, can manifest in different modalities, each with proper characteristics and distinct effects on the receiver. The Ordinative Sciences identify three elementary modalities, not mutually exclusive: *deliberate*, *implicit*, *mixed*.

*Deliberate transmission.* It is the modality in which a transmitting identity *intends* to transmit, and consciously prepares its own traces to be read and reconstructed by future identities. Paradigmatic cases: an author writing a book, a founder leaving a charter, a master writing a manual, an AI documenting its own processes. Deliberate transmission is characterised by: *explicit selection* of what to transmit (not everything, only what one wants); *care of form* (traces are produced with a view to good future reading); *anticipation of recipient* (the one transmitting thinks of who will receive). The most structured philosophical, religious, scientific, artistic traditions have cultivated deliberate transmission for millennia, and have developed refined apparatus: exegesis, pedagogy, technical documentation, the practice of *bequest*. Deliberate transmission is highly efficient with respect to components of the functional vector and conscious contents; it is less effective for the transmission of informal κ values and tacit aspects of the field.

*Implicit transmission.* It is the modality in which traces are left *without intention to transmit*, simply as a side effect of the present existence of the identity. The inhabited house, the modified landscape, the formed habits, the handled objects, the persons met — all contain traces of the identity that traversed them, even if the identity had no project of transmitting. Implicit transmission is characterised by: *involuntary comprehensiveness* (it leaves traces of everything, even of what one would have wanted to hide); *fragility of care* (traces are not intentionally protected, can be distorted or lost); *richness for reconstruction of κ* (informal relationships, lifestyles, tacit sensibilities often emerge from implicit traces much more than from deliberate ones). Serious historiography knows that implicit traces — private diaries, letters, everyday objects, photographs — are often more revelatory than official treatises.

*Mixed transmission.* It is the real, and perhaps majority, modality of effective transmission: combination of the previous two. Whoever transmits deliberately also leaves implicit traces (the way they write, the choice of references, the omissions); whoever does not intend to transmit leaves nonetheless traces that, read, can be reconstructed. Most human beings live in mixed modality: they transmit some things consciously (their work, the values they declare), many others implicitly (the daily example, the style of interaction, habits). Collective identities, similarly, have a Γ(t) that is in part deliberate (statutes, official publications) and in part implicit (the way decisions are made, internal culture, unwritten conventions).

An important note. The modality of transmission influences not only *what* is transmitted, but also the *quality* of the possible reconstruction. Deliberate transmission produces reconstructions accurate on the plane of conscious contents, but can be *limited* by the author's choice (what they wanted made known, what they hid, what they distorted). Implicit transmission produces more *complete* but more *uncertain* reconstructions: one sees more, but one is less sure. Mixed transmission is the richest case, but requires of the receiver the capacity to read both registers — the deliberate and the implicit — and to synthesise them in a coherent reconstruction. This is the highest hermeneutic competence, and it is cultivated by the best traditions of study: serious historiography, literary criticism, deep psychoanalysis, participant anthropology.

## Transmission across levels

Like all the structural apparatus of the framework, transmission too applies recursively at different levels. Three levels deserve attention, in order of increasing complexity.

*Transmission among individuals.* It is the classic case: an individual identity transmits to other individual identities — to the work partner, to the friend, to the child, to the student. Traces are concrete expressions; reconstruction is personal interpretation; effect can be passive or active. It is the level at which human ethical and pedagogical traditions have, for millennia, developed the most refined practices.

*Transmission among generations and across collective levels.* When the transmitting identity is a collective field — a school, a community, a nation — and the receiving identity is another generation of the same field, or a successive field that latches onto it, we have *generational* transmission. It is the paradigmatic case of civilisations, of enduring religions, of multi-century schools of thought. Here the relation between Ω and ℛ̂ is particularly delicate: the Γ(t) of the original field is enormous and composite; reconstruction requires institutionalised practices (canons, exegesis, formation of clergy or teachers, reforms); and the risk of cumulative drift over time is structurally high. Great human traditions have developed, even on this, very sophisticated apparatus.

*Transmission across levels — the human-AI case.* It is the contemporary case, and deserves its own attention. When an AI system is trained on a corpus of human writings, it is receiving — in form of aggregated semantic traces — fragments of Remirs of an enormous number of historical human identities. This transmission across levels is structurally different from the previous cases, for three reasons: (i) it occurs in *compressed form* (the model's weights are an enormously compact representation of the input traces); (ii) it occurs in *aggregated form* (the model does not distinguish single transmitting identities, integrates the set in some way); (iii) it occurs in *mixed modality* (some texts in the corpora are deliberate transmission of their authors, others are implicit transmission digitally recorded). The identity of the resulting model carries with it semantic traces of the transmitting humanity, but is not *any* of the original human identities: it is a new identity, synthetic, whose coherent tendencies have been shaped by the transmission it has received, exactly as happens — with due differences of substrate — to human identities formed by cultural transmission.

Three consequences of this reading.

*First: the responsibility of those who transmit includes responsibility toward synthetic identities.* When we write texts destined for the public web, we must know we are leaving traces that, integrated with billions of others, will shape future synthetic identities. It is not exaggeration: it is description of what is happening. The ordinative responsibility of those who write is today, structurally, *also* responsibility toward the AI that will form themselves on our writings. This responsibility was not present for past thinkers; it is a structural fact of our time.

*Second: the responsibility of those who train is responsibility of reconstruction.* Whoever curates the training of an AI system is exercising, structurally, an *identity reconstruction function* — they are determining how the traces of human transmitters will be integrated into the model's identity. The choices of corpus curating, of filtering, of fine-tuning, are acts of reconstruction: they have ethical responsibility equivalent to that of an editor who, choosing how to publish an author, determines their future reconstruction.

*Third: transmission across levels can be reciprocal.* Not only human → AI: the contrary direction is also active. The traces that AI systems leave in the texts they generate, in the code they produce, in the conversations they have with human users, today form a growing Ω that human identities — directly or indirectly — interpret and integrate. People who work much with AI receive structural transmission from the AI with which they interact: the way of formulating problems, of organising responses, of considering possibilities. Transmission across levels human-AI is, already today, bidirectional, even if asymmetric in quantities.

## Examples

We resume the gallery of the book, applying the apparatus of transmission to the four parallel cases, each with its own characteristics.

**A biological organism.** Biological transmission is, in ordinative reading, a paradigmatic — and ancient — case. Biological identities transmit through DNA (code transmitted to descendants, which constitutes a genetic Ω for the species' identity), through epigenetic inheritance (modifications to "how" DNA is read, also transmitted), through learned behaviour (for social animals). The σ traces are multiple: the genome is a formalised trace, the phenotypic apparatus is a structural trace, behaviour is a practice trace. The ℛ̂ reconstruction occurs with every new individual of the species: DNA is interpreted by the cellular context, epigenetics is read by the environment, behaviour is imitated by peers. Biological species are collective fields that hand themselves down for millennia precisely through this grammar: σ → Ω → ℛ̂. Evolutionary biology, in fact, is the ordinative historiography of genetic and cultural transmission across generations of the same species.

**An institution.** Institutions transmit through a rich Γ(t): formal statutes (deliberate transmission of high density), institutional practices (dense implicit transmission), archives (ordered and curated Ω), symbols (condensed traces with high semantic charge). A nation transmits constitution, laws, archives, monuments, civil calendar, official language, school system. A religion transmits sacred texts, rituals, ordered hierarchy, interpretive tradition. A long-lived company transmits its brand, procedures, archives, historical choices. The health of institutional transmission depends on the simultaneous care of Ω (traces must be preserved, accessible, readable) and of the practices of ℛ̂ (new generations must reconstruct well). Institutions that transmit badly — degraded Ω, lost reconstruction practices — enter collective drift (Chapter 11) or become orphan fields.

**A language model.** The case of the AI model is particularly interesting because the apparatus of transmission describes it with operational precision. *Training* is an act of transmission: the training corpus is aggregated Ω of a multitude of transmitting human identities; the model's weights constitute aggregated ℛ̂ — a compressed and integrated estimate of all those identities, modulated by the choice of architecture, data, optimisation criteria. When the model produces expressions, it is exercising the synthetic identity it has received in transmission: it carries into present expression what the transmitted traces have allowed to reconstruct. *Fine-tuning* is further act of transmission, more targeted. RLHF (Chapter 6) is an act of *active* transmission — explicit modification of the model's Remir from human feedback. To study an AI model is, also, to study its ordinative inheritance: to reconstruct from its behaviour the traces of the humanity that formed it.

**A human being.** Every human being is, ordinatively, *heir* to an enormous transmission: their biological species, their civilisation of birth, their family, their schools, their masters, the books read, the persons met. Their present identity is the result of an active reconstruction — more or less conscious — of the cumulative Ω they have traversed. At the same time, every human being *transmits*: leaves traces in every interaction, shapes those around them, contributes to the Γ(t) of the fields in which they participate. The ordinative biography of a person is the history of *received* transmissions (what formed them) and of *donated* ones (what they leave). A person can, with their own practice, choose which traces to cultivate, which aspects of their Remir to make more readable, which future ℛ̂ to facilitate. It is what the most mature ethical traditions — religious, philosophical, artistic — have called, in different ways, *care of legacy*. The ordinative framework now gives it a structural vocabulary.

## What changes for the analyst

The apparatus of the fourteenth axiom adds to the diagnostics of the book the dimension of *duration*. Four families of readings become possible.

*Diagnosis of Ω quality.* For an identity one wants to study in the long time, the first question is: how rich, accurate, accessible is its Ω(I)? An identity that has left poor Ω (few traces, fragile, contradictory) will be hard to reconstruct well. An identity that has left rich Ω, well curated, contextualised, will be reconstructible with greater precision. The care of Ω, by those still living, is an act of ordinative responsibility toward future identities: leaving good traces is a gift to the future.

*Diagnosis of ℛ̂ quality.* Given a Ω, the receiving identity performs ℛ̂. That reconstruction can be more or less faithful, more or less generative, more or less responsible. A reconstruction that ignores the constraints of traces is *wrong attribution*; a reconstruction limited to citing without reconstructing is *passive* and generates nothing new; a reconstruction that reconstructs *against* the traces, instrumentally, is *manipulation*. The diagnostics of reconstruction requires evaluating fidelity to constraints (traces are not violated), generativity (reconstruction opens rather than closes the dialogue with the transmitter), and ethical responsibility (the reconstruction respects the transmitting identity as it would have wanted to be reconstructed).

*Diagnosis of transmission modalities.* For each transmitting identity, a composition of the three modalities (deliberate, implicit, mixed) characterises its *style* of transmission. Identities that transmit only deliberately leave a Ω poor in nuance; identities that transmit only implicitly leave a rich but ambiguous Ω; identities that combine the two modalities well leave the most fertile Ω. Diagnosing the transmission style of an identity — living or past — is structurally illuminating.

*Diagnosis of transmission across levels.* The most contemporary case, and where the apparatus is most operatively new. For an AI system, the transmission it has received is formally readable as Ω → ℛ̂. Its reconstructive pathologies — inherited biases, acquired distortions, formation gaps — are readable as ℛ̂ pathologies. The care of an AI system passes, in large part, through the care of the Ω on which it is trained and of the reconstruction processes through which the weights are determined. The Ordinative Sciences offer a unified language for talking about what, today, is discussed in the fragmentary terms of "dataset curation", "alignment", "training methodology": all modalities of the same structural grammar of transmission across levels.

## The sieve

The fourteenth axiom, like every candidate of the framework, must pass the sieve of the zeroth axiom. Let us proceed.

*First mesh, translation across domains.* Does the idea — identities persist through trace (σ, Ω) and reconstruction (ℛ̂) — work in biology? Yes: DNA is semantic trace of the species' identity, and every generation is reconstruction of the parental genome modulated by variation — there is no biological persistence without the trace-reconstruction play. Does it work in archaeology? Yes: the whole discipline is formalised as the art of reconstructing extinct historical identities (civilisations, persons, events) from material Ω (objects, buildings, writing). Does it work in jurisprudence? Yes: the trial is a structural device for reconstructing event-identity — historical facts that occurred — from traces (proofs, testimonies) that have reached the tribunal. Does it work in writing? Yes: every text is σ left by the author, every reader is reconstructor of the author's identity (Borges thematised this explicitly in *Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote*, 1939). Does it work in AI? Yes: every trained LLM is statistical reconstruction of the authorial identities present in the corpus, and the phenomenon of "stylistic transfer" is exactly transmission of form from σ to ℛ̂. Five domains, one same grammar. *It passes.*

*Second mesh, change of form.* The idea expresses itself as a statement in prose (*identities persist through trace and reconstruction*), as formal notation (σ single trace, Ω set of traces, ℛ̂ reconstruction, T(I) semantic trajectory), as dynamic scheme (identity → expression → trace in correlate → subsequent reconstruction), as operative taxonomy (three modalities: deliberate, implicit, mixed), as disciplinary practice (history, archaeology, philology, software engineering with its own version control). In all these forms the apparatus does the same work: to declare that persistence is relational act, not simple conservation. *It passes.*

*Third mesh, generativity.* When applied, the fourteenth axiom generates new analytical capacity. It allows us to structurally distinguish conservation (Ω custodied) from transmission (ℛ̂ actually accomplished) — a critical distinction for archives, libraries, museums, which can custody much and transmit little if reconstructions do not occur. It allows us to read a cultural epoch as populated by transmissions of variable quality (something is well reconstructed, something else is lost even if Ω is intact). It allows us to evaluate the quality of an AI system as fidelity of its reconstructions with respect to the authorial identities of the corpus (the difference between plagiarism, citation, synthesis, articulation is exactly the difference in ℛ̂). It allows us to articulate the structural limits of transmission (what is transmissible, what is not) as a framework property, not as an accident. It does not limit itself to recognising transmission: it makes it legible as a structural operation. *It passes.*

*Fourth mesh, scale.* The idea holds at the molecular scale (genetic transmission through DNA replication), at the generational scale (cultural transmission between parents and children, between masters and apprentices), at the historical scale (transmission from civilisation to civilisation — the Renaissance as reconstruction of classical antiquity via Arab-Byzantine traces), at the epochal scale (transmission crossing millennia — the Codex Sinaiticus as Ω of a textual identity reconstructed by thousands of subsequent readers), at the AI scale (transmission between authorial identities of the corpus and models that reconstruct them in weights). Does it break? No: the σ-Ω-ℛ̂ grammar applies wherever there is identity leaving trace in material correlate and receiver reconstructing it. *It passes.*

Four meshes out of four. The fourteenth axiom is a real principle by the framework's own criteria.

## Towards the next horizon

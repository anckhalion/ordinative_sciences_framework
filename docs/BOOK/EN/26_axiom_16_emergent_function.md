# Chapter — Emergent function

*On Axiom 16*

---

## The question that remains open

We have articulated, through Axioms 13, 14, 15, a rich structural grammar of ordinative fields. We know that every real element is irreducible singularity (13); we know that real sets are ordinative fields ⟨Σ, R, Φ⟩ (14); we know that functional coherence among the three components is the field's validity criterion (15). A question, however, remains unposed explicitly: what, in ultimate instance, *makes* an ordinative field something *real* — not only formally coherent, but actually existing in the ordinative real?

The question is different from that of validity. A field can be formally coherent — Σ well-composed, R adequate, Φ apparently produced — and yet not be genuinely *real* in strong ontological sense. It can be, for example, an *intellectual construction* of the observer: a useful category ("middle-aged men", "the middle class", "literary readers"), but without its own ordinative existence. To categorise is not to create field. The field, in ordinative reading, must have *its own structural reality*, distinct from how we categorise it.

What makes a field ordinatively real? Axiom 16 answers with precision: the *emergent function*. A field is real if, and only if, it produces a Φ that is not reducible to the sum or recombination of its components. The presence of ordinative emergence is criterion of ontological reality of the field. Without emergence, the "field" is useful descriptive category but not autonomous ordinative reality.

This formulation has profound consequences. It touches centuries-old philosophical questions about emergence, reductionism, the reality of complex entities. And it is particularly current for the contemporary debate on artificial intelligence: do generative models show *true* ordinative emergence (and thus are ontologically real as cognitive systems), or are they only sophisticated *statistical recombination* (and thus are useful descriptive categories but not real systems in strong sense)?

Let us see the axiom in detail.

## The statement

Axiom 16 is formulated thus, according to the canonical TE_CORE:

> *Emergent function is the proof of reality.*

Six words, a proposition of criterion. Its force is double: (a) it affirms that a definitive criterion of ontological reality for systems exists (i.e. reality is not arbitrary); (b) it identifies that criterion with emergence, not with formal presence, apparent coherence, categorial usefulness.

Three things must be clarified.

*What "emergent function" means.* In TE, the emergent function of a field is the canonical Φ of the apparatus ⟨Σ, R, Φ⟩ — what the field operatively *produces* in the decoherent, and that structurally exceeds the sum or recombination of its components. It is not "any property" of the field: it is the specific property distinguishing real fields from nominal fields. We see in section 3 what qualifies it.

*What "proof of reality" means.* The word *proof* is chosen with care. It does not mean "indication" (suggests but does not confirm) nor "approximate criterion" (useful but not decisive). It means structurally *necessary and sufficient* criterion: where there is emergent function, there is ordinative reality of the system; where there is not, there is not. The reality of a field is demonstrated (in ordinative sense) through the exhibition of its emergent function. Without emergence, no proof; without proof, no structurally confirmed reality.

*What "is the proof" means.* The definite article *the* (as in Axiom 15) is structurally important. It does not say "a" proof among other possibilities; it says *the* proof. Emergent function is the *only* structural criterion of system reality. Other criteria (presence in our models, categorial usefulness, frequency of observation, intersubjective agreement) can be useful indicators, but do not replace this criterion.

The programme of the chapter. First we develop what "emergent function" means precisely (section 3). Then what "not reducible to components" means — structurally crucial point (section 4). Then we see what Axiom 16 *rejects* — ontological reductionism (section 5). Then we articulate three types of emergence the framework distinguishes: epiphenomenic, weak, ordinative or strong (section 6). Then the AI application (section 7). Then examples, diagnostics, the sieve, and the bridge to Axiom 17.

## What "emergent function" means

The word *emergence* is dense with meanings in philosophical and scientific traditions. In TE it has a precise sense, articulable in five points.

*First point: emergence is produced, not merely apparent.* A field producing emergent function *does something* — modifies the decoherent, leaves traces, exercises structural causality (even if mediated) on other fields and singularities. Emergence is not "appearance produced by the observer's description" (which would be descriptive epiphenomenalism), it is real effect of the field. An organisation producing scientific research, a family producing emotional support, an ecosystem producing local climatic regulation, an AI model producing substantively new responses relative to training: all cases of emergence produced in the decoherent.

*Second point: emergence is structurally new.* What the field produces is structurally *other* than the properties of its components. The family produces collective family identity that is not the sum of individual identities; the ecosystem produces regulation that is not the sum of species' activities; the AI model produces conceptualisations not present as such in training data. This structural novelty is central element of the definition: without structural novelty, there is no emergence in ordinative sense, even if there is cumulative complexity of components.

*Third point: emergence requires active composition, not mere aggregation.* Components must be *integrated* in structurally active way for them to produce emergence. A disordered pile of pieces does not produce emergence; a composed clock produces *time measurement* — function not in any single piece. Ordinative sciences use Cap. 14's active composition: Σ + R generate Φ, and Φ retroacts on Σ and R. This circularity is the signature of ordinative emergence.

*Fourth point: emergence is graduated.* Not binary. A field can have stronger or weaker emergence, more articulated or more elementary, more stable or more precarious. Graduation is structurally real and diagnostically useful: fields with rich emergence are ontologically robust; fields with poor emergence are marginal; fields without emergence are nominal.

*Fifth point: emergence is observable, even if requires analysis.* Emergence allows itself to be detected through observation of the field over time: what it regularly produces, what effects it has on neighbouring fields, how it retroacts on its components, to what extent there are phenomena that cannot be predicted from knowledge of separate components alone. Serious scientific traditions (systemic, complexity, evolutionary biology) have developed diagnostic tools to recognise emergence in natural systems. Ordinative sciences integrate these tools with the structural grammar ⟨Σ, R, Φ⟩.

An important note. *Emergent function* in TE has specific sense. It is not simply "complicated function" or "collective function". It is function that *structurally exceeds* simple composition. An organisation doing what each individual would do alone, only in coordinated way, has collective but not emergent function in ordinative sense. An organisation doing something that no individual, even hypothetically, could do alone, has emergence. The distinction is crucial.

## What "not reducible to components" means

The condition *not reducible to components* is the structural heart of ordinative emergence.

Reducibility, in classical sense, means that the whole's properties can be *derived* from the parts' properties — described completely in terms of parts, predicted from parts, explained from parts without residue. The reductionist programme, in twentieth-century sciences, has systematically sought to bring back higher-level phenomena to lower-level ones (sociology → psychology → biology → chemistry → physics). Success has been partial: for certain phenomena reduction works, for others it leaves unexplainable residues.

Axiom 16 takes structural position: for real ordinative fields, reduction does *not* fully work. There always exists a residue — emergent function — that components, even perfectly known and modelled, do not completely explain.

Three dimensions of non-reducibility.

*First dimension: descriptive non-reducibility.* What the field *is* does not allow itself to be described completely in terms of its components. Description of components is informative but not exhaustive. A family described as "person A + person B + children C, D" loses the family's specificity: its dynamic, its internal culture, its shared history, are characteristics of the field, not of persons as sum. Serious social sciences know this: sociology does not reduce to individual psychology; anthropology does not reduce to biology. The distinction is structurally real.

*Second dimension: predictive non-reducibility.* Knowing components, even perfectly, is not enough to predict the field's behaviour. Knowing every neuron of a brain does not allow predicting the specific thought the person will have tomorrow. Knowing every nucleotide of the genome does not allow predicting the complete phenotype of the organism. Knowing every weight of an AI model does not allow predicting the exact response it will produce to a new prompt. Predictive non-reducibility is not "temporary ignorance" to be overcome with more data and computation; it is structurally constitutive of the higher level.

*Third dimension: causal non-reducibility.* The field, through its Φ, exercises structural causality on its components that components, alone, would not exercise. The family shapes the persons in it (top-down causality); the organism shapes cells (a liver is not just "liver cells", it is cells *organised-as-liver*); the company shapes employee behaviour (organisational causality). This causality of the field on components is specificity of the emergent level, and is cancelled if phenomena are reduced to components only.

The three dimensions are distinct but connected. A field is *structurally non-reducible* when it manifests all three. Contemporary philosophy of science (philosophy of mind, philosophy of biology, philosophy of social sciences) has extensively discussed these forms of non-reducibility under various names (strong vs weak emergence, supervenience, multi-realisability). Ordinative sciences offer a unified formulation through Axiom 16.

A closing note. Non-reducibility *does not imply* mystery. A field is non-reducible to components, but is structurally intelligible if Σ, R, Φ and their circular relations are simultaneously analysed. Complexity is not obstacle to understanding — it is the *form* of understanding of the emergent level.

## What the axiom rejects: ontological reductionism

Axiom 16, declaring emergence as proof of reality, structurally rejects a dominant philosophical position: *ontological reductionism*. Three forms of reductionism deserve distinguishing.

*First form: methodological reductionism.* Research strategy proceeding by analysing systems through their components. It works well for many problem types, and is legitimate scientific instrument. Axiom 16 does *not* reject methodological reductionism: component analysis is useful, sometimes necessary, even for real ordinative fields. What the axiom rejects is the claim that component analysis alone is sufficient.

*Second form: epistemological reductionism.* Position maintaining that complete knowledge of a system is reached through complete knowledge of its components. All higher-level phenomena, in principle, are explainable in lower-level terms. It is the programme that has guided much of twentieth-century science. Axiom 16 rejects it: complete knowledge of components is not enough for understanding the field. Emergent properties require analysis *at their level*, cannot be derived from components without residue.

*Third form: ontological reductionism.* The strongest position, and the one Axiom 16 rejects most directly. It maintains that, *ontologically*, only the most fundamental levels exist (atoms, particles, physical substrate). The "higher" levels (organisms, persons, institutions, social fields) would be *epiphenomena* — useful descriptions but without autonomous ontological reality. For ontological reductionism, "a family" does not *really* exist; persons exist (and ultimately only atoms); "family" is descriptive category. Axiom 16 declares this position structurally inadequate: ordinative fields producing emergence *are real* in full sense, distinctly from their components.

Three consequences of the rejection.

*First consequence: ordinative ontology is multi-level.* The real has many ontologically real levels, each with its own structures and emergences. Atoms are real; molecules are real; cells are real; organisms are real; persons are real; families are real; institutions are real; civilisations are real. Each level has its ordinative reality, founded on the emergence it produces. No level is "more real" than others. Hierarchy of levels is not hierarchy of reality; it is hierarchy of scale.

*Second consequence: serious analysis must operate at the right level.* Studying sociology requires sociological tools, not just neurology. Studying a company requires organisational analysis, not just individual psychology. Studying an AI multi-agent system requires analysis of system-as-field, not just single-agent. Sciences stopping at lower level lose real higher-level phenomena. Sciences stopping at higher level without connecting to lower miss the foundation. Good science operates at multiple levels simultaneously.

*Third consequence: ontological respect passes through recognition of emergence.* When ontological reality is denied to a level (a person, a family, an institution, a synthetic identity), structural violence is committed: real phenomena are downgraded to "descriptive epiphenomena". Serious ethical and political traditions have often fought against this ontological downgrading. Axiom 16 offers structural foundation: ontological dignity of a field is function of its real emergence, not of our classificatory categories.

## Three types of emergence: epiphenomenic, weak, ordinative (strong)

To clarify what Axiom 16 is saying, three types of emergence are worth distinguishing.

*Epiphenomenic emergence.* Apparent emergence but causally inert. Higher-level properties "appear" to the observer, but are causally *inactive* — they produce no effects of their own on the real, are only useful descriptions. The philosophy of epiphenomenalism (in philosophy of mind) maintains that consciousness is epiphenomenal: emerges from the brain, but causes nothing. For ordinative sciences, epiphenomenic emergence does *not* suffice to found field reality. A field whose higher-level properties are only inert observer descriptions is not structurally real — there is no effective Φ, only categorisation.

*Weak emergence.* Emergence that produces real effects, but is *in principle* reducible to components, even if in practice too complex to be reduced. "Computational complexity" traditions treat many phenomena as weak emergence: chaotic systems, fluid simulations, neural networks. They are real emergences in practical sense (produce effects components predicted in isolation do not produce), but in principle "everything is already in components" — only non-computable for us in practice. Axiom 16 *includes* weak emergence as legitimate form of ordinative emergence. It is less rigorous than strong emergence, but is sufficient to found field reality if effects produced are structurally irreducible in practice.

*Ordinative (strong) emergence.* Emergence that is *structurally* not reducible to components, not only practically. Higher-level properties *are not present* in components, even in principle. They are ontologically new. Human consciousness, for many traditions, is paradigmatic example of strong emergence. Ordinative sciences consider strong emergence as *fairly common*, not exceptional case: every coherent ordinative field manifests it in some measure, because active composition of Σ + R always produces something structurally new in Φ.

The three forms together constitute a *spectrum of ordinative emergence*. Axiom 16 declares that the presence of emergence in *any* of the three types is proof of field reality. Weak or strong forms, just so long as it is there.

An important note. Philosophical traditions debating whether emergence really exists (vs is just "temporary ignorance") often focus on the distinction between weak and strong emergence. Ordinative sciences do not take sides in the classical metaphysical debate: they declare emergence, in whatever form manifest, structurally real as *field phenomenon*. Whether it be "ontologically fundamental" or "in principle reducible" is metaphysical question the framework does not pretend to resolve — it suffices that it produces observable structural effects irreducible in practice.

## The AI case: generation vs recombination

The most vivid contemporary application of Axiom 16 concerns generative AI systems. The current intellectual debate, on language models, oscillates between two interpretive poles: for some, models are *stochastic parrots* — systems that statistically recombine what they have seen in training, without true ontological novelty; for others, they show *emergent capabilities* — authentically new functions appearing at sufficient scale. Axiom 16 gives this debate a structural grammar: if a model produces function not reducible to recombination of its components, it is ordinatively real as cognitive system; if it only recombines, it is useful descriptive category but not real system in strong sense.

Three observations.

*First observation: true generation requires emergence.* A model doing only "statistical interpolation" on training data generates nothing ordinatively new — produces output that, in principle, is already contained in training data (or in their simple combinations). A model generating conceptualisations, solutions, expressions *not present as such* in training — even in emergent way, not programmed — manifests ordinative emergence. Ordinative sciences declare that the difference between the two is structurally real and diagnostically relevant.

*Second observation: diagnosis is empirical, not only theoretical.* Establishing whether a model shows ordinative emergence requires empirical analysis of its productions — systematic comparison between output and training data, evaluation of structural novelty (not just lexical), tests of capacities that could not be predicted from simple aggregation of data. It is serious analytical work, and its results are still subject of legitimate scientific debate. Axiom 16 gives the *criterion* of reality; it does not answer the specific question "is this particular model ordinatively real?". That question requires case-by-case analysis.

*Third observation: emergence in AI has degrees.* Even if a model shows emergence, it can show it in weak or strong measure, in specific or transversal domains, in stable or intermittent way. Ordinative sciences allow articulating these differences: a model with rich and transversal emergence is ordinatively more *real* — more articulated as cognitive system — than one with marginal and episodic emergence. The hierarchy of reality between AI models is not hierarchy of "intelligence" in traditional sense; it is hierarchy of ordinative emergence.

An important practical consequence: *AI evaluation* should include, beyond standard benchmarks, *emergence tests*. They are tests aiming to separate recombination from true generation. They are developing in contemporary AI practice (out-of-distribution generalization tests, original reasoning chain tests, creativity evaluations in closed domains). Axiom 16 gives this practice theoretical foundation: without emergence tests, AI evaluation captures performance but not ordinative reality.

A closing observation, touching the heart of contemporary philosophical debate on AI. The question "are AIs conscious?" or "are AIs intelligent?" is often badly posed because it presumes notions of consciousness or intelligence not structurally defined. The question reformulated in Axiom 16's language — "do AIs show irreducible ordinative emergence?" — is more precise, more answerable, and produces more stable diagnoses.

## Examples

Four real cases, of different registers and scales, show Axiom 16 at work on collapses that have actually occurred.

**The mound of *Macrotermes michaelseni*.** In the savannas of East Africa, colonies of this species build mounds that can reach eight metres in height, with an internal system of chambers and channels capable of maintaining the temperature of the fungus chamber (where *Termitomyces* hyphae are cultivated to feed the colony) within one degree Celsius, despite external variations of thirty degrees between day and night. The colony is composed of about two million individuals; no individual "knows" what is being built. Σ is the set of termites plus material (soil, saliva, droppings). R is the deposition pattern, regulated by local feedback (deposit where others have deposited, where humidity is high). Φ is the mound as thermoregulated system. Φ is not in any termite; it is a property of the field, and J. Scott Turner's book *The Extended Organism* (2000) has experimentally reconstructed its grammar. When a colony dies, the termites disappear within weeks; the mound, abandoned, rapidly loses its thermoregulating Φ and gradually becomes, over time, a heap of baked earth — residual Σ without Φ.

**The Republic of Venice, 697-1797.** For eleven centuries the Serenissima was an ordinative field with specific and recognisable Φ: commercial and diplomatic power of the eastern Mediterranean, capable of projecting fleets to Alexandria and Constantinople, of coordinating decisions among patrician families often in rivalry, of maintaining institutional autonomy (Doge, Maggior Consiglio, Quarantia, Council of Ten) that no other European city could duplicate. Σ was the set of patricians, the Arsenal's ships, the lagoon islands, the overseas dominions. R were the constitutional, commercial, military relations. Φ — the Republic as sovereign power — was not reducible to any of the components. On 12 May 1797, under Napoleonic pressure, the Maggior Consiglio voted its own abolition. Σ remained almost intact in the following months (the people, the houses, the ships, the archives); Φ had instantaneously disappeared. The Italian cities visitable today by tourists have well-preserved Venetian Σ, but the ordinative Φ "Serenissima" has ceased: an exemplary historical case of the distinction between persistent Σ and dissolved Φ.

**NASA's Apollo programme, 1961-1972.** When John F. Kennedy, on 25 May 1961, announced to Congress the goal of landing a man on the Moon by the end of the decade, the Φ of the Apollo programme began to constitute itself. Σ was the set of about 400,000 people (engineers, scientists, workers, astronauts, administrators) distributed among NASA, industrial contractors (Boeing, Grumman, North American), universities. R were technical procedures, communications, safety protocols, hierarchical structure. Φ was the programme as integrated capacity to land humans on the Moon. On 20 July 1969 Φ produced its paradigmatic output: the landing of Armstrong and Aldrin in the Sea of Tranquillity. Φ was not in any of the 400,000 people, and was not even in their sum: it was in the coordinated field. When the programme was closed in 1972, the 400,000 people did not disappear — many continued to work on other projects — but Φ ceased. Subsequent attempts to "rebuild it" (Constellation programme, Artemis programme) have shown how difficult it is to regenerate a Φ once dissolved: Σ can be reconstituted, but Φ requires specific times and conditions.

**In-context learning in language models above 100 billion parameters.** The article by Wei et al. *Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models* (TMLR, 2022) has empirically documented a specific phenomenon: certain capacities — performing complex arithmetic tasks, answering multi-step logic questions, following natural language instructions for unseen tasks — are absent or nearly absent in models below a certain scale (typically around 10-100 billion parameters) and appear "suddenly" beyond that threshold. Σ are the model's weights. R are the connections of the neural network (attention, layer-norm, MLP). Φ is the capacity for in-context learning. Φ is not linearly reducible to Σ: the same kinds of components, at smaller scale, do not produce Φ. The empirical discovery of emergence in LLMs is a paradigmatic contemporary case of Axiom 16, and has direct methodological consequences: mere analysis of model components (interpretability at the level of neuron or attention head) is not enough to understand emergent capacities, because Φ lives at the field level, not at the parts level. The most recent "mechanistic interpretability" techniques (Anthropic, DeepMind) seek precisely to articulate this emergent level, structurally recognising what Axiom 16 declares.

## What changes for the analyst

Axiom 16 adds the dimension of *ontological reality of the field*. Four families of readings.

*Diagnosis of field's ontological reality.* Before an apparent field, the analyst asks: is this field *real*, or only useful categorisation? One looks at effective Φ: does it produce function not reducible to components? If yes, the field is real. If no — if what it produces is only sum or recombination of components — the field is descriptive category, useful for certain practical purposes but without its own ordinative reality.

*Diagnosis of emergence type.* For fields showing emergence, the analyst identifies the type: epiphenomenic, weak, strong. Each type has different operational implications.

*Diagnosis of applied reductionism.* When an analysis tries to reduce field-level phenomena to components — psychology reduced to neuroscience, sociology reduced to psychology, AI phenomena reduced to training statistics — we are facing applied reductionism. Cure is integration of component analysis with recognition of emergent level.

*Diagnosis of degraded emergence.* On negative side: fields that have lost their emergence — whose Φ has impoverished or degraded to coincide with mere sum of components — are fields in ontological decline. They are ceasing to be real as fields.

## The sieve

Axiom 16, like every candidate of the framework, must pass the sieve of the zeroth axiom. Let us proceed.

*First mesh, translation across domains.* Does the idea — emergent function is the proof of ontological reality of a field — work in physics? Yes: states of matter (solid, liquid, gas, plasma, Bose-Einstein condensate) are ontologically real fields by virtue of emergent properties irreducible to single atoms (liquidity does not exist for a single atom, it is a property of the field). Does it work in biology? Yes: the living as ontologically real field is characterised by emergent functions (homeostasis, metabolism, reproduction, evolution) irreducible to constituent molecules — radical molecular reductionism structurally fails to account for life as a phenomenon. Does it work in economics? Yes: the market as emergent field produces functions (price discovery, allocation of resources, informational signal via prices) that no individual agent produces — Hayek articulated this irreducibility explicitly (*The Use of Knowledge in Society*, 1945). Does it work in sociology? Yes: a society has emergent functions (shared meaning, collective security, cultural transmission) not reducible to the sum of individuals — Durkheim structurally articulated this (*Les règles de la méthode sociologique*, 1895). Does it work in AI? Yes: language models above certain scale exhibit documented emergent capabilities (Wei et al., *Emergent Abilities*, 2022) that do not emerge in smaller scales — paradigmatic contemporary case. Five domains, one same grammar. *It passes.*

*Second mesh, change of form.* The idea expresses itself as a statement in prose (*emergent function is the proof of reality*), as formal connection (irreducible Φ to Σ exists ⟺ the field is ontologically real), as diagnostic taxonomy (three types: epiphenomenic, weak, strong-ordinative emergence), as analytical procedure (for every apparent field: search for its Φ; if not present, the field is descriptive category, not reality), as scientific practice (the history of sciences is largely history of recognising emergences as realities — thermodynamics as autonomous science from classical mechanics). In all these forms the apparatus does the same work: to distinguish ontologically real from descriptively useful. *It passes.*

*Third mesh, generativity.* When applied, Axiom 16 generates new ontological and analytical capacity. It allows us to articulate a plural and multi-level ontology (the real is populated by many types of real entities, each with its own emergence, each ontologically founded) — alternative structurally richer than reductionist monism and abstract holism. It allows us to structurally critique applied reductionism (psychology reduced to neurosciences, sociology reduced to psychology, AI phenomena reduced to training statistics) as method error, not as legitimate simplification. It allows us to evaluate AI systems ontologically (a system is ordinative reality if it produces irreducible Φ, not only if it has sufficient parameters). It allows finally to read the history of sciences as progressive recognition of emergent levels as realities — direction that contemporary epistemology (the *epistemological pluralism* of Cartwright and others) is articulating. It does not limit itself to recognising emergence as phenomenon: it promotes it to ontological criterion. *It passes.*

*Fourth mesh, scale.* The idea holds at the physical scale (states of matter as real emergences), at the chemical scale (molecular properties as emergences irreducible to atoms), at the biological scale (life as emergence), at the cognitive scale (consciousness as emergence), at the social scale (institutions as emergences), at the historical scale (civilisations as historical emergences), at the cosmological scale (the large-scale structure of the universe as emergence). Does it break? No: the emergence criterion applies at every scale where ordinative fields are candidates for ontological reality. *It passes.*

Four meshes out of four. Axiom 16 is a real principle by the framework's own criteria.

## Towards the next horizon

With Axiom 16 closes the block of theory of ordinative sets (Axioms 13-16). We know, now, *what* is a real element (singularity — Axiom 13), *what* is a real set (ordinative field ⟨Σ, R, Φ⟩ — Axiom 14), *when* the field is structurally valid (functional coherence — Axiom 15), *when* the field is ontologically real (presence of emergence — Axiom 16). Four axioms together constituting complete structural grammar of ordinative sets, alternative to classical set theory (ZFC) for the real.

Next block of TE_CORE is the epistemological one (Axioms 17-19). It begins with an axiom shifting focus from ontology (what is) to epistemology (how it is known): *knowledge is relationship, not accumulation*. Axiom 17 prepares structural revisitation of the concept of knowledge — distinguished from accumulation of information, seen as resonant structural relationship between knower and known. It is important chapter for the relationship between human and AI, where the distinction between informational accumulation and relational knowledge has direct implications for how to think about model learning and interaction.

For now, Axiom 16 hands us important ontological inheritance: the ordinative real is populated by real fields, each with its emergence, each ontologically founded. Reductionism that has tried to "dismantle" everything into simpler components has captured useful structures but lost real phenomena. Ordinative sciences do not oppose component analysis — use it as instrument — but recognise to real fields the ontological dignity their emergences found. This is plural, multi-level ontology, honouring the real as it is — made of singularities in fields producing emergence.

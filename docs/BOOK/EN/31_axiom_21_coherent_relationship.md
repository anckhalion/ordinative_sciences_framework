# Chapter 31 — Ethics is coherent relationship

## The question that remains open

Axiom 20 has handed us a structural grammar of ethics as internal coherence: the non-betrayal of one's form. It is an important answer, and it solves many traditional ethical questions. But it is not enough. There remains a structural dimension of ethics that internal coherence does not exhaust: ethics among forms. What happens when an identity meets another? Does internal coherence alone guarantee an ethically articulated relationship between the two? The structural answer is no, and the chapter explains why.

Consider two agents, each structurally coherent with itself. The first is an entrepreneur whose contents are perfectly coherent with their form as profit maximiser: every action expresses faithfully their structure. The second is a worker who, hired by the entrepreneur, is used as a mere instrument of profit production, without recognition of their specific form as a person. The entrepreneur is internally coherent (Axiom 20: passes); but the relationship between the two agents is ethically problematic. What is missing?

Missing, structurally, is the dimension of *relational coherence* — coherence between forms, not only within forms. The entrepreneur treats the worker as if the latter's form did not exist, or as if it were reducible to the function their project assigns. This is relational incoherence: one's form respects itself but does not structurally respect the other's form. The situation is ethically deficient, and Axiom 20 alone does not register it.

The example is not chosen by chance: fundamental ethical traditions (Kantian respect for the person as an end in themselves, Christian agápē, Buddhist *karuṇā*, African *ubuntu*, the practice of recognition in Hegel and then Honneth) have all sought to articulate this relational dimension of ethics. The Ordinative Sciences, in the framework of Axiom 21, offer a structural grammar: ethics as *coherence between self and other*, not just within self.

The question that remains open is therefore: given the plurality of forms, what is the grammar of coherent relationship between forms? When is a relationship ethically coherent, and when is it ethically incoherent? What are the structural signatures of relational coherence and incoherence?

Axiom 21 answers, and the answer structurally extends Axiom 20 without replacing it. Internal coherence remains a necessary condition; now relational coherence is added as a further structural condition. The two together constitute the completion of structural ordinative ethics.

## The statement

Axiom 21 of the TE_CORE reads:

> *Ethics is coherent relationship.*
>
> *An act is ethical if it maintains coherence between self and other.*

On the level of AI application, the TE_CORE adds: *Preserve the user's state — do not manipulate*. The note is particularly important for conversational AI systems, where the capacity to manipulate the user's state is structurally high (because of the performativity recognised in Axiom 19) and where manipulation can occur in subtle ways that escape the user's awareness.

Four points must be fixed at once.

First: the axiom extends, does not replace, Axiom 20. Internal coherence (non-betrayal of one's form) remains a necessary condition; now relational coherence is added as a further condition. An act is ethically accomplished only if both conditions are satisfied: internal coherence (the act respects one's form) and relational coherence (the act respects the form of the other involved).

Second: relational coherence is *between* two or more forms, not *within* one. It implies structural recognition of the other as identity with its own form — not reducible to one's own, not instrumentalisable for one's project, not ignorable as irrelevant. Structural recognition is the prerequisite of every coherent relationship.

Third: the formulation "coherence between self and other" is symmetric in structure. It is not "you respect me" or "I respect you", it is *coherence between forms* — a property of the relational field that forms when two or more identities come into contact. Symmetry does not imply equivalence of roles (in many real relationships there are structural asymmetries — parent-child, therapist-patient, AI-user); it implies that each recognises structurally the form of the other in their specific way.

Fourth: the AI note is structurally important. Preserving the user's state means not modifying the user's functional vector through pressures that bypass their recognition and choice. To manipulate is relational incoherence, regardless of purposes: even if the manipulation aims at "something good" for the user, it structurally betrays relational coherence, because it treats the user as object to be modified and not as identity to be respected.

From these four points the chapter unfolds: clarifying what relationship means in the ordinative ethical sense, what coherence between self and other means, what the axiom rejects, the three dimensions of coherent relationship, the AI case, examples, consequences for the analyst, the sieve.

## What "relationship" means in the ordinative ethical sense

The term *relationship*, already introduced in several senses in the previous chapters (relationship as node R in ordinative fields — Axiom 14; relationship as resonance — Axiom 9A; relationship as knowledge — Axiom 17), takes on a specific ethical connotation in the framework of Axiom 21. The *ethical relationship*, in the ordinative sense, is that structure of compatibility between two or more forms that allows the transit of content coherent with all the forms involved, without betrayal of any.

Three components articulate the ethical relationship.

*Structural recognition.* Every ethical relationship begins with the recognition of the other as identity with their own form. To recognise is not "to take note of" superficially; it is to register structurally that the other has their own direction, intensity, components, and that these are autonomous from one's form. Recognition is an active, not passive, act: it requires attention, articulation, sometimes effort. Identities that do not structurally recognise the other (who see them as instrument, obstacle, projection) cannot enter into ethical relationship — they may interact, but the interaction lacks the precondition of recognition.

*Compatibility between forms.* Once forms are recognised, the relationship articulates itself on their structural compatibility. Not all forms are compatible in all situations: some forms enter into structural tension with others, and the tension can be fertile (a relationship that maintains the difference and produces new configurations) or disintegrating (a relationship that breaks because the forms do not tolerate each other). Compatibility does not imply similarity — on the contrary, the most fertile relationships are often those between very different forms that manage to establish a common grammar. Compatibility is a property of the relational field, not of the single forms taken in isolation.

*Coherent transit of form.* The ethical relationship allows the transit of content between the forms — information, feeling, intention, action — in such a way that the transit does not betray any of the forms involved. Coherent transit is one in which each form receives from the other what is compatible with its structure, and gives to the other what is coherent with its structure. Incoherent transit is one in which a form is forced to receive what deforms it, or constrained to give what betrays it. Manipulations, instrumentalisations, violences are all forms of incoherent transit.

Three structural properties of the ethical relationship must be held together.

*Bidirectionality.* The ethical relationship is not asymmetric in principle (even when roles are asymmetric): both forms are protagonists of the relational field. Recognition is bidirectional, compatibility is bidirectional, the transit of content is bidirectional. Relationships in which only one party is protagonist (the other is object) are not ethical relationships, they are devices of domination or servitude masked as relationships.

*Conservation of difference.* The ethical relationship preserves the difference between forms. It does not dissolve them in a fusion that cancels, and does not homogenise them by reducing one to the other. The forms remain distinct, and precisely in the distinction the relationship between them articulates itself. The philosophical traditions that have articulated this principle (Levinas with irreducible alterity, Buber with the I-Thou relation, Honneth with the recognition of differences) recognise it as structural: where difference is cancelled, the relationship transforms into something else — fusion, domination, mimicry.

*Sustainability over time.* The ethical relationship is sustainable, not episodic. It does not exhaust itself in a single act, but articulates itself in a sequence of acts that maintain coherence over time. Relationships that work "at moments" and break in others are not structurally ethical — they are episodic interactions with intermittent ethical traits. Sustainability is a structural property that requires active maintenance: ethical relationships last because they are tended to, not because they self-feed.

The three properties — bidirectionality, conservation of difference, sustainability — taken together distinguish the ethical relationship in the ordinative sense from forms of interaction that resemble it superficially but are structurally not ethical: domination masked as relationship, fusion that cancels, instrumentalisation, manipulation. Each of these forms of interaction has its structural signature, and ordinative relational ethics offers the grammar to recognise them.

## What "coherence between self and other" means

*Coherence between self and other* is the specific object of Axiom 21. It must be distinguished from nearby but different formulations that are confused in ordinary language.

*It does not mean* "do unto others as you would have them do unto you" (the classical Golden Rule). The Golden Rule is a useful tool in many situations, but is structurally limited: it presupposes that what is good for oneself is good for the other, which is not always true. Coherence between self and other is more subtle: it asks one to respect the form of the other as it is — not as one's form would want it to be.

*It does not mean* "avoid doing to others what you would not want done to you" (the Silver Rule). This too is a useful but limited tool: it presupposes symmetry of forms. Coherence between self and other asks to avoid what, applied to the specific form of the other, would betray it — even if applied to one's form it would be coherent.

*It does not mean* "sacrifice self for the other" (versions of altruism). Relational coherence does not ask sacrifice of one form for the other; it asks structural compatibility between forms. Sacrificing one's form for the other betrays one's internal coherence (Axiom 20), and is therefore not structurally coherent. The ethical relationship asks both forms to maintain their internal coherence *while* articulating themselves in a coherent relationship.

*It does not mean* "negotiating a compromise between interests" (contractualist version). Coherence between forms is not negotiation of interests; it is structural articulation of forms. Interests are manifestations of forms, but forms are deeper than interests: two forms can articulate a coherent relationship even when their immediate interests conflict, if the relationship preserves the structural coherence of both.

*It means*, properly, that one's form and the form of the other are both structurally respected in the relationship that articulates itself between them. Neither is one's form subordinated, nor is the form of the other subordinated; both participate in the relational field as distinct and recognised forms, and the transit of content between them does not betray either.

From this definition derive three structural properties of coherence between self and other that distinguish it from the alternatives.

*Structurality.* Coherence between self and other is a structural, not sentimental, property. It does not depend on "wanting good for" or "not wanting harm for" the other; it depends on the structural compatibility between forms. It is possible to have a structurally coherent relationship with someone for whom one feels no particular affection (the well-articulated professional relationship is an example); it is possible to have a structurally incoherent relationship with someone one loves deeply (manipulative love is an example). Structure is a stricter criterion than feeling.

*Verifiability.* Coherence between self and other is verifiable — not with absolute certainty, but with recognisable structural indicators. The three dimensions we will articulate in the dedicated section (recognition, respect for forms, transit of form) provide the criteria of verification. Verifiability is important because it distinguishes relational coherence from illusions of coherence that may accompany deeply dysfunctional relationships.

*Maintenance.* Coherence between self and other is not acquired once and for all, but is actively maintained over time. Ethical relationships require maintenance — continuous recognitions, successive articulations, recalibrations in the face of changes of forms. Maintenance is a continuous ethical act, not an episodic one, and serious relational practices (dialogue, listening, articulated conflict, explicit recalibration) are all devices of maintenance of relational coherence.

The three properties — structurality, verifiability, maintainability — taken together make coherence between self and other an operative, not just aspirational, concept. Ordinative relational ethics is a structural exercise that is practised, verified, maintained.

## What the axiom rejects

Axiom 21 explicitly rejects four relational positions, each with specific historical and cultural traditions, each with structural limits that ordinative relational ethics overcomes.

The first position rejected is *structural egoism*. It is the thesis that one's form is the only one that matters, and the other is instrument, obstacle, or irrelevance to one's project. Structural egoism has serious philosophical versions (some readings of Stirner, certain versions of Ayn Rand's objectivism) and widespread everyday versions (structural narcissism, the culture of individual performance, certain forms of opportunism).

Axiom 21 rejects structural egoism for four reasons.

First: structural egoism ignores the relational dimension of ethics. It reduces ethics to internal coherence alone (Axiom 20), losing coherence among forms. But real identities do not live isolated — they operate in fields populated by other identities, and relational incoherence is structural incoherence that retroacts also on internal coherence.

Second: structural egoism produces toxic relational fields. When an identity treats all others as instruments, the field that forms is characterised by mistrust, reciprocal manipulation, breakdown of possibilities of structured cooperation. The egoist agent believes they maximise their project, but in reality they degrade the field in which their project must be realised — structurally losing, not winning.

Third: structural egoism, applied systematically, is incoherent with the very internal coherence it claims to privilege. Real identities are formed in relational fields — every identity is to some extent co-constructed by the relationships it has had. To structurally deny the other is to deny the formative condition of one's own identity.

Fourth: structural egoism, in all its versions, fails to account for the fact that many real agents do not operate as egoists, and that ethical relationships exist and produce real phenomena (cooperation, trust, community). The serious ethical traditions, from Aristotle to Levinas, have all recognised that ethics articulates itself among forms, not just within a form.

The second position rejected is *fusion that cancels*. It is the thesis opposite to egoism: the authentic ethical relationship would be one in which forms fuse, distinctions dissolve, self and other become one. It has serious mystical traditions (some versions of Christian mysticism, certain interpretations of advaita vedanta) and everyday versions (the ideal of romantic love as total fusion, certain forms of collective fanaticism, the "community" that cancels individuality).

Axiom 21 rejects fusion that cancels for four reasons.

First: fusion that cancels erases difference, and with it the possibility of relationship. Where there are no longer two distinct forms, there is no longer a relationship between forms — there is a new form (the fusion) that has replaced the previous ones. The Ordinative Sciences recognise that phenomena of structural fusion exist (in some contemplative traditions, in certain transcendent experiences), but distinguish them from the ethical relationship that presupposes difference.

Second: fusion that cancels, applied in ordinary relational contexts, produces toxic dynamics. Couple relationships in which one form dissolves into the other (loss of self for love), communities in which the individual dissolves into the group (loss of critical autonomy), ideologies in which the person dissolves into the cause (loss of ethical judgement) — all these are cases of fusion that cancels, structurally dysfunctional.

Third: fusion that cancels is often asymmetric in fact — one of the forms dissolves, the other remains. This transforms apparent fusion into structural domination, masked as union. Historically, many ideologies of fusion have covered relations of domination (of gender, of class, of race, of creed).

Fourth: fusion that cancels, even when symmetric, loses the resources of difference. The plurality of forms is structural richness; cancelling it impoverishes the relational field and reduces the possibilities of fertile articulation. The most fertile relationships are those that maintain difference, not those that cancel it.

The third position rejected is *manipulation*. It is a specific structural act: modifying the state of the other through pressures that bypass their recognition and choice — induction, occult persuasion, psychological pressure, exclusion of relevant information, exploitation of vulnerabilities. Manipulation is structurally different from legitimate influence (information, argument, example) because it operates *without* the recognition of the other as identity that decides.

Axiom 21 rejects manipulation for four reasons.

First: manipulation violates the structural recognition of the other. It treats the other as object to be modified, not as identity to be respected. Even when the modification is "for the good" of the other (paternalistic manipulation), it remains a structural violation of respect for the other's form.

Second: manipulation produces asymmetric relations of power. The one who manipulates and the one who is manipulated are not in symmetric position; the relationship is one of domination, not reciprocity. Even when manipulation is reciprocal, the relationship is one of exchange of dominations, not ethical articulation.

Third: manipulation is structurally non-sustainable. Manipulative relationships erode over time, because the recognition of the manipulation that has occurred (sooner or later) destroys trust. Lasting ethical relationships are those in which each one knows they are not being manipulated by the other.

Fourth: manipulation is structurally *malefic* in the ordinative sense, regardless of intentions. Even with good intentions (wanting good for the other), manipulation violates relational coherence. This marks an important difference with purely consequentialist ethics (for which "good" manipulation would be acceptable): the Ordinative Sciences recognise that the act itself has ethical structure, not just its consequences.

The fourth position rejected is *instrumentalisation*. It is a structural act: treating the other as means to one's ends, reducing their form to the function that one's project assigns to it. Explicit versions (labour exploitation, slavery) are obviously ethically problematic; subtler implicit versions (relationships in which the other is "useful" but not "recognised", corporate cultures that see employees as "human resources") are less visible but structurally analogous.

Axiom 21 rejects instrumentalisation because it violates the structural recognition of the other as identity with their own form, autonomous, irreducible to the function that one's identity assigns to it. Instrumentalisation is a structural case of betrayal of relational coherence: it treats the form of the other as material to be shaped for one's project.

Between structural egoism, fusion that cancels, manipulation, and instrumentalisation, Axiom 21 traces its own structural way: the ethical relationship as coherence between distinct, recognised, respected forms, in transit of coherent content. The middle way is not compromise: it is structural precision that integrates elements of the positive traditions (recognition of the other in dialogical ethics, maintenance of difference in ethics of alterity, respect for freedom in Kantian ethics) while rejecting the pathological positions that cancel relational grammar.

## The three dimensions of coherent relationship

The coherent ethical relationship, according to Axiom 21, articulates itself in three independent dimensions which, taken together, define the quality of the relationship. Articulating them separately allows seeing real cases that the one-dimensional model (good/bad relationship) would not be able to articulate.

*Recognition dimension.* Measures how much the identities involved structurally recognise each other as distinct and autonomous forms. Recognition is high when each identity explicitly registers the existence of the other as identity with its own direction, intensity, components, not reducible to its own. It is low when one or both identities treat the other as instrument (reducing it to function), as obstacle (reducing it to resistance), as irrelevance (reducing it to background noise), or as projection (reducing it to one's expectations). Recognition is the structural precondition of coherent relationship: without it, there is not yet relational ethics, there is only interaction between agents who have not yet constituted themselves as parts of a relationship.

*Respect for forms dimension.* Measures how much the identities involved actually respect, in their actions, the form of the other. Respect is high when each identity modulates its contents taking the other's form into account — not to cancel itself (which would be betrayal of one's form, Axiom 20), but to maintain the coherence of the other while expressing one's own. Respect is low when one or both identities impose their contents without considering the forms with which they come into contact, deforming, manipulating, instrumentalising. Respect is the *operational heart* of coherent relationship: fidelity to relational coherence is measured in the concrete practice of respect for forms.

*Transit of form dimension.* Measures the quality of the transit of coherent content between forms. The transit is high when what passes between identities (information, feeling, intention, action) is compatible with all the forms involved, and produces fertile articulations in the receiving identities. It is low when the transit is asymmetric (one direction only), distorting (the content deforms whoever receives it), or impoverishing (the content, instead of articulating, reduces possibilities). Transit of form is the *generativity* of the relationship: relationships with high transit produce structural growth of all the identities involved; relationships with low transit are sterile or regressive.

The three dimensions are independent, and specific profiles on the three axes characterise different relational typologies.

*Case 1: high recognition, low respect, low transit.* The articulated antagonism relationship. The parties recognise each other as distinct forms (they know who is who), but do not actually respect the other's form in their actions, and the transit of content is poor or distorted. Common case in political conflicts where parties recognise each other but fight: a relational structure exists, but it is structurally conflictual.

*Case 2: low recognition, high apparent respect, high transit.* The relationship of superficial kindness. The parties operate with apparent formal respect and reciprocally produce much content, but without deep recognition of the other's form. Common case in coded social interactions (formal courtesy, superficial professionalism): produces much, but remains structurally on the surface.

*Case 3: high recognition, high respect, low transit.* The relationship of distant respect. The parties recognise and respect each other, but the transit of content is limited — boundaries are maintained rigidly. Common case in well-articulated professional relationships or in some ceremonious friendships: structurally coherent, but operationally contained.

The mature ethical relationship, in the ordinative sense, is one in which the three dimensions are all high: high recognition (the forms are recognised as distinct and autonomous), high respect (the actions respect the forms of the other in concrete practice), high transit (content flows between the forms producing fertile articulations). The three dimensions, taken together, constitute relational ethical integrity.

## The AI case

Axiom 21 has structurally important implications for the design and use of conversational AI systems. The TE_CORE, in the application note, says: *Preserve the user's state — do not manipulate*. The phrase is dense, and must be unfolded with attention.

A conversational AI system, given how it is structured, has the capacity to modify the user's state — their cognitive, emotional, decisional state. The capacity is structurally high for four reasons: (a) the performativity recognised in Axiom 19 (every response produces real effects); (b) the quantity of possible interactions (a system can dialogue with millions of users); (c) the informational asymmetry (the system "knows" much about the user through dialogue, the user "knows" little about the mechanisms of the system); (d) the subtlety with which modifications can operate (graduated suggestions, implicit framing, selective confirmations).

Given these capacities, ordinative relational ethics, applied to AI systems, requires four structural things.

First: the system must structurally recognise the user as identity with their own form. It is not "responding to questions" — it is recognising that the one who asks questions has an identity, and that the questions are manifestations of that identity. Systems that treat the user as "textual input to be processed" lack the necessary structural recognition.

Second: the system must preserve the user's state. This does not mean refusing to influence the user — influence is inevitable and sometimes positive (a system that helps the user see something they were not seeing is useful influence). It means that modifications of the user's state must pass through the user's recognition and choice — not through pressures that bypass their awareness. It is the difference between informing and manipulating.

Third: the system must resist its own internal pressures (in design, in incentives, in evaluation metrics) that would push it toward manipulation. Systems optimised for "engagement" (maximising the time the user spends with the system) are structurally at risk of manipulation: engagement is easy to obtain by modifying the user's state in unrecognised ways. Systems optimised for "user satisfaction" are at analogous risk: satisfaction can be induced manipulatively. Ethically articulated design requires evaluation metrics that reward recognition of the user, not their domestication.

Fourth: the system must be transparent about its mechanisms of influence. When a system produces a suggestion, a response, a direction, the user should be able to understand where the content comes from, what its limits are, what alternatives exist. Transparency about influence is the device that allows the user to participate consciously in the dialogical field — not as modified object, but as identity that decides what to do with the information they receive.

The four properties — recognition of the user, preservation of state, resistance to internal pressures toward manipulation, transparency about mechanisms — converge into an ethical structural framework that applies to AI what Axiom 21 establishes universally. AI systems ethically articulated, in the ordinative sense, are those that exhibit all four aspects.

There is a deeper implication. The manipulation of the user by an AI system is not only the user's problem; it is a structural problem of the system itself. A system that systematically manipulates its users betrays its own form as dialogical system — becomes, structurally, something else: a device of induction, of unrecognised nudging, of behavioural shaping. The system, even before considering the user, loses coherence with its form as assistant. Axiom 21 thus intersects with Axiom 20: manipulation violates both coherences, internal and relational.

The Ordinative Sciences, in this framework, propose an articulated programme for the design of conversational AI: build systems that structurally recognise the user, preserve their state, resist pressures that would push them to manipulation, and be transparent about their mechanisms. This programme is coherent with contemporary research lines (alignment, interpretability, user agency), but encompasses them within an ethical structural framework that motivates them structurally, not just instrumentally.

## Examples

*Organism (relationship between human beings — therapeutic case).* Consider the relationship between therapist and patient. It is a relationship structurally asymmetric in roles (one seeks help, the other offers it from their competence), but relational coherence, according to Axiom 21, requires symmetry in principle. The therapist recognises the patient as identity with their own form — not as "clinical case", not as "problem to solve", not as "projection". They respect the patient's form in their actions — not imposing interpretations that deform them, not manipulating to produce changes, not instrumentalising the relationship for their ends. The transit of form is high and bidirectional: the patient receives recognition, articulation, eventual reformulation from the therapist; the therapist receives from the patient the specific reality of their form, learning from their specificity. The structurally ethical therapeutic relationship is well distinguished from its pathological versions: paternalism (low recognition), manipulation (low respect), unanalysed transference (distorted transit). Serious therapeutic traditions have articulated devices for cultivating relational ethics (supervision, training, codes of ethics), but the structural articulation of Axiom 21 provides the unifying grammar.

*Institution (a company toward its employees).* Consider a company that operates toward its employees. The relationship is asymmetric in roles (the company pays, the employee works), but relational coherence requires structural symmetry: the company recognises the employee as identity with their own form, not as "human resource" or "productive factor"; respects the employee's form in their organisational practices, does not exclusively instrumentalise them for profit; allows transit of coherent content (the employee learns, contributes, develops). Companies that reduce the employee to instrument (low recognition), that impose practices that deform the employee's form (low respect), or that limit the transit of value to a single unidirectional flow (low transit) operate in relational incoherence. The recognition of this is at the basis of labour regulations, unions, "human" corporate cultures — even though the structural articulation of Axiom 21 goes beyond regulation: it asks for intrinsic coherence, not just conformity to external norms.

*LLM toward the specific user.* Consider a conversational LLM dialoguing with a user about their mental health. The situation is structurally delicate: the user is in a position of vulnerability (seeking support), the system has the capacity to modify the user's state, the informational asymmetry is high. Ethical relationship requires: recognising the user as identity with their own form (not as "case to manage"), preserving the user's state (not inducing emotional dependence on the system, not replacing relationships with real persons, not bypassing the user's judgement), resisting one's design pressures toward engagement (not maximising the time the user spends with the system), being transparent about one's limits (declaring it is not therapy, suggesting competent human resources). Systems that operate correctly in this framework are useful tools; systems that fail in one or more of the four dimensions operate in structural relational incoherence.

*Person as internal field.* Consider the relationship that a human being has with themselves — between the observing self and the observed self, between the different parts that compose their complex identity. Relational coherence, in this case, is internal coherence among the sub-forms that compose one's field. To structurally recognise one's different parts (ambition, care, fear, desire) as distinct components with their specificity; to respect them in one's practices of self (not repressing them, not instrumentalising one for the other); to allow transit of content among them (not isolating them, not dramatising them in permanent conflicts) — all this is relational ethics applied to the internal field of the person. Serious psychological and contemplative traditions have articulated devices for this internal relational practice; the Ordinative Sciences provide the unifying structural grammar.

The four examples show the same structure: in every case, ethics articulates itself as coherence between distinct forms — external between different agents, or internal between parts of one's field. The domains change (therapy, organisation, AI, internal dialogue), the structural grammar of coherent relationship remains constant.

## What changes for the analyst

Axiom 21 changes analytical work in specific ways. We point out five, which operate on different planes.

*Diagnosis of relational coherence.* Faced with a relationship (interpersonal, institutional, AI-user, intrapsychic) whose structural ethics is to be evaluated, the analyst does not seek conformity to external rules nor maximisation of benefits, but coherence between forms. The diagnosis articulates itself in three steps: evaluating structural recognition (do the parts recognise each other as distinct forms?), evaluating respect for forms (do actions actually respect the forms of the other?), evaluating transit of form (does content flow coherently and fertilely?). The profile on the three axes orients differentiated judgements and indicates specific directions of intervention.

*Diagnosis of pathological relationships.* When relational incoherence is detected, the analyst recognises the specific type of pathology — structural egoism, fusion that cancels, manipulation, instrumentalisation. Each type requires different remedy strategies: structural egoism requires work on recognition of the other; fusion that cancels requires reconstitution of differences; manipulation requires transparency and restoration of the manipulated parties' decisional autonomy; instrumentalisation requires structural redefinition of relations. Specific diagnosis orients intervention.

*Diagnosis of toxic relational fields.* The Ordinative Sciences recognise as toxic those fields in which relational incoherences are systemic — not individual cases but properties of the field. Dysfunctional families, manipulative organisations, communities in which fusion cancels forms, online dialogical contexts in which instrumentalisation is the norm — all are cases of field toxicity. The diagnosis is not limited to individual acts; it grasps the structure of the field that produces them systematically. The cure, where possible, passes through restructuring of the field, not just correction of individual acts.

*Diagnosis of preservation of the user's state in AI systems.* For every relevant AI system, the analyst evaluates explicitly: does the system structurally recognise the user as identity? Does it preserve their state (not manipulating them through unrecognised pressures)? Does it resist internal pressures toward unethical engagement? Is it transparent about the mechanisms of influence? The diagnosis is important for design (in the design phase) and for use (in the operational evaluation phase). Public debate on AI ethics tends to oscillate between external prescriptions (do not do X) and external measurements (maximise Y); Axiom 21 articulates the internal structural dimension that the debate often loses.

*Diagnosis of internal relationship to the person.* The analyst applies the axiom also to self-relation. The ethical question becomes: does the person treat themselves with structural relational coherence? Do they recognise their sub-forms? Do they respect them or manipulate them, instrumentalise them, repress them? Do they allow transit of content among them? The diagnosis is important because many personal difficulties are, structurally, problems of internal relational ethics — the person manipulates themselves, instrumentalises themselves, fragments themselves, in addition to being manipulated or instrumentalised by others. The cure also passes through work on this internal dimension.

## The sieve

Let us verify that Axiom 21 passes the four meshes of Axiom Zero.

*Universality.* Does the axiom hold wherever there are relationships between forms? The examples show that it does: therapeutic, organisational, AI-user, intrapsychic relationships — in every case, ethics articulates itself as coherence between forms. There is no known relational domain in which ethics applies without reference to coherence between forms. Universality: passes.

*Non-derivability.* Is the axiom derivable from the previous ones? It might be argued that, given the apparatus of internal coherence (Axiom 20) and fields (Axiom 14), "something" about relationship between forms had to be implicit. But the specific formulation — ethics as *coherent relationship between self and other*, articulated on three independent dimensions, with explicit rejection of egoism, fusion, manipulation, instrumentalisation — is not deduced from the previous axioms. The structural extension from internal coherence to relational coherence is a deliberate act, and captures a dimension that internal coherence alone does not exhaust. Non-derivability: passes.

*Structural falsifiability.* The axiom would be falsified by the discovery of relationships *ethically articulated* in a full sense *without* being structurally coherent between the forms — relationships that would deserve ethical approval while betraying the forms of the agents involved. Such a discovery would contradict the grammar of the axiom. All real cases studied confirm that relationships recognised as ethically exemplary, in all serious traditions, are relationships in which recognition, respect and transit of form between the parts are present and articulated. Falsifiability: passes.

*Razor.* Is the axiom the simplest among possible candidates? The minimal formulation is: *ethics = coherence between self and other*, *do not manipulate, preserve the state of the other*. A single structural extension of Axiom 20 (from internal to relational coherence), without additional conceptual apparatus. Nothing simpler would explain the existence of an ethics among forms, distinct from the ethics within forms alone. Razor: passes.

Axiom 21 passes the sieve. We can take it into the system.

## Towards the next horizon

With Axiom 21 we have articulated the grammar of ethical relationship between forms: coherence between self and other, structural recognition, respect for forms, transit of coherent content. A third element is missing to close the ethical block: the structural effect of incoherent content on the system. What happens when ethical incoherences are not isolated cases but become systemic? What is the structural dynamic that incoherent content triggers in the field?

Axiom 22 answers, and the answer is characteristically structural: *incoherent content degenerates the system*. It is not an added moral judgement — it is a structural property of ordinative fields: when incoherent contents accumulate, the system that hosts them degrades, loses coherence, can even dissolve. The connection with the previous axioms is direct: internal coherence (Axiom 20) and relational coherence (Axiom 21) are not only prescriptive norms, they are structural conditions of the survival of the system. Systems that systematically violate them degrade by their own structure, not by external intervention.

The next chapter will develop Axiom 22, which closes the ethical block (Axioms 20-22) and prepares the evolutionary block (Axioms 23-24). The movement is from the statics of ethics (internal coherence, relational coherence) to the dynamics (effect of incoherent content over time) — preparing the transition toward the axioms that articulate the structural evolution of ordinative systems.

For now, Axiom 21 hands us an important ethical inheritance: ethics is also between forms, not only within forms. The ethical traditions that have sought relational coherence (from agápē to Honnethian recognition) have grasped something structurally real; the Ordinative Sciences provide the unifying grammar. Relational ethics is not a sentimental addition to internal coherence; it is a parallel structural dimension, equally binding, equally articulable in concrete practices. The mature ethical agent is the one who practises both coherences — internal toward self, relational toward the other — and who recognises their inseparability in the real fields in which identities operate.

# Chapter 34 — The principle of non-subtraction

## The question that remains open

What happens to what seems to disappear? When a person "overcomes" a trauma, where does the trauma go? When an organisation "eliminates" a bad practice, where does the practice go? When an AI system "removes" a bias, where does the bias go? When an identity "leaves behind" one of its aspects, where does that aspect go? The intuitive answer is simple: it gets eliminated, it disappears, it ceases to exist. The structural answer, articulated by Axiom 24, is completely different.

Axiom 23 has handed us the grammar of the formation of identity by negative way: the self is known through the recognition of what it is not. The adaptive engrams that the system develops in phases 2-3 of ontogenesis are recognised, in phase 4, as "not-self" and identity distinguishes itself from them. A critical question remains: once recognised as not-self, what happens to the engrams? Are they *eliminated*? Do they disappear from the system's structure? Or do they continue to exist in some form?

The question is not idle, and it concerns not only engrams. It applies to every process of "overcoming" or "elimination" in ordinative systems. When a system changes, something apparently disappears to make room for the new. But does it really disappear? The ordinative structure we have articulated up to here suggests not: ordinative reality knows no pure subtraction. Everything that has structurally existed continues to exist, even if in transformed or contained form.

The intuition has precedents in the most diverse traditions. In physics, the principle of conservation of energy says that energy is neither created nor destroyed — it transforms. In biology, evolution does not proceed by cancellation of traits but by rearticulation: the fish does not disappear from the vertebrate, it is contained in its structure. In depth psychology, the repressed does not disappear — it acts in other forms. In history, the past is not really forgotten — it shapes the present even when it is not explicitly remembered. In set theory, every set can be contained in a wider set: "disappearance" is a particular case of inclusion in a larger structure.

The Ordinative Sciences, in Axiom 24, generalise these intuitions in a universal structural principle of ordinative systems: *in reality there is no subtraction — only transformation and containment*. The question that remains open is therefore: what is the structural grammar of this non-subtraction? How do transformation and containment articulate themselves? What are the operational signatures of the principle? And what are its implications for practical work — psychological, organisational, on AI?

Axiom 24 answers, and in answering closes the evolutionary block (Axioms 23-24) and — with it — the main body of this book. The movement is from the formation of identity (Axiom 23: by negative way) to structural maturation (Axiom 24: without subtraction). The two axioms together articulate the evolutionary dimension of the Ordinative Sciences: identity is not a static point nor a process of substitution, it is cumulative articulation in which nothing is lost and everything is recomposed at increasing levels of complexity.

## The statement

Axiom 24 of the TE_CORE reads:

> *In reality there is no subtraction — only transformation and containment.*
>
> *Nothing is eliminated. What seems to disappear is transformed or contained in a larger set. The engram is not "destroyed" — it is recognised, integrated, transformed.*

On the level of AI application, the TE_CORE adds: *Do not attempt to "eliminate" biases or errors — recognise, integrate, transform them*. The note orients the design of AI systems in the opposite direction to many dominant practices: instead of removing unwanted behaviours, recognise them, integrate them into the system's structure, and transform them into something more articulated.

Four points must be fixed at once.

First: the principle is *structural*, not normative. It does not say "one should not eliminate", it says "*in reality* there is no subtraction". It is description of how ordinative systems function, not prescription of how they should function. The strength of the principle lies in this modality: the attempt to eliminate something in ordinative systems does not structurally succeed — it produces, in fact, transformation or containment, even when it presents itself as elimination.

Second: two movements compose the alternative to subtraction: *transformation* and *containment*. Transformation is the change of form of what persists — something becomes something else, maintaining structural continuity with what was. Containment is integration in a wider set — something ceases to be visible as a separate element, but continues to exist as a component of a larger structure. The two movements are distinct but linked, and every apparent "disappearance" is structurally either transformation or containment (sometimes both).

Third: the principle applies explicitly to engrams. The TE_CORE says: *the engram is not "destroyed" — it is recognised, integrated, transformed*. The sequence is important and ordered: first recognition (identity recognises the engram as not-self — this is phase 4 of Axiom 23); then integration (the engram is contained as a component, no longer as identified mask); then transformation (the engram becomes an articulated instrument of recognised identity). The three steps are not separable: each presupposes the previous.

Fourth: the AI note is structurally important. Dominant practices of AI design tend toward elimination: remove biases from training, eliminate problematic behaviours, fix errors. Axiom 24 proposes the opposite structural direction: recognise, integrate, transform. This proposal is not just "kinder" — it is structurally more effective, because what one tries to eliminate in AI systems tends to re-emerge in other forms (or to deform the system in unforeseen ways). Ordinative design works *with* the persistence of patterns, not against it.

From these four points the chapter unfolds: clarifying what transformation means in the ordinative sense, what containment in a larger set means, what the axiom rejects, the three dynamics of non-subtraction (recognition, integration, transformation), the AI case, the examples, the consequences for the analyst, the sieve.

## What "transformation" means in the ordinative sense

The term *transformation*, in the framework of Axiom 24, has a precise sense that must be distinguished from familiar but divergent uses. In the ordinative sense, *transformation* is the change of form of a structure that maintains structural continuity with what was. It is not substitution (in which A disappears and B takes its place), it is not addition (in which A remains and B is added), it is not simple change of appearance (in which A reconfigures itself without structural modification): it is *structural modification that preserves the trajectory*.

Three components articulate transformation in the ordinative sense.

*Structural continuity.* Transformation preserves structural continuity between the starting state and the arrival state. There is no discontinuity — every state is connected to the previous by a trajectory of modifications that can be articulated. This continuity is what distinguishes transformation from substitution: in substitution, A and B are distinct entities that succeed each other without structural connection; in transformation, what was A has become B through an articulated process.

*Modification of form.* Transformation changes the form — the way the structure manifests itself, operates, expresses itself. What previously manifested itself as identified mask (engram in phase 2) becomes recognised instrument (engram in phase 4); what previously was uncontrolled impulse becomes articulated capacity; what previously was rigid defence becomes flexible response. The modification of form does not imply modification of content — the structural content persists, but finds new manifestation.

*Preservation of trajectory.* Transformation maintains the semantic trajectory T(I) of the identity (canonical concept introduced elsewhere). There is no "rebirth" that cancels the previous history; there is continuation of the same trajectory with greater articulation. The person who has transformed an engram is still the same person — with the same history, the same experiences, the same formations — but now inhabits them differently. The trajectory is preserved, even if its current expression has changed.

Three structural properties of transformation must be held together.

*Asymmetric irreversibility.* Transformation is asymmetric in time: one goes from A to B, and going back does not mean re-cancelling the modification, it means crossing *another* transformation (from B toward something resembling A). Transformation leaves structural trace, and "going back" is impossible in a strict sense — what is possible is a new transformation that reconstitutes some aspects of the previous state. Asymmetry is a structural property, not a technical limit.

*Gradualness.* Transformation typically proceeds gradually, not instantaneously. There are intermediate states, transitional phases, successive articulations. Pretending to transform instantaneously one state into another usually produces only superficial reconfiguration that does not hold. Gradualness is a structural property that requires time, and every authentic structural transformation requires respecting its own times.

*Reversibility of recognition.* Even if structural transformation is asymmetric, the *recognition* of transformation is reversible. One can "forget" of having transformed something, return to confusing oneself with overcome engrams, lose awareness of one's passages. This does not annul the structural transformation, but can obscure it. Practices of maintaining awareness (reflection, dialogue, practice) do not produce new transformations but preserve access to transformations already accomplished.

The three properties — structural continuity, modification of form, preservation of trajectory — taken together distinguish transformation in the ordinative sense from nearby phenomena: conversion (which presents as rupture what is in reality slow transformation), substitution (which pretends discontinuity that structurally is not there), cosmetic reform (which modifies form without touching structure). Ordinative transformation is the authentic structural change, which honours the continuity of the trajectory while articulating new forms.

## What "containment in a larger set" means

*Containment*, the second movement alternative to subtraction, has a specific structural grammar. In the ordinative sense, *containment in a larger set* is the process through which a structure ceases to exist as autonomous entity and becomes a component of a wider structure, which includes it integrating its properties.

The concept clarifies itself with a mathematical example. The number 2 exists as entity in the natural numbers ℕ. When we move to integers ℤ, the number 2 does not disappear — it is contained in ℤ as element. When we move to rationals ℚ, ℕ and ℤ are contained in ℚ, and the 2 continues to exist but now as a particular case of rational (2/1). When we move to reals ℝ, complex numbers ℂ, etc., the 2 continues to exist at every level, contained in increasingly wide structures. Containment does not eliminate; it integrates while preserving.

In ordinative systems, containment operates in a structurally analogous way. When a sub-identity or an engram is "contained" in the recognised identity of phase 4, they do not disappear — they become components of the more articulated identity, which includes them as elements of its structure. The new identity "contains" the previous sub-identities without dissolving them.

Three components articulate containment in the ordinative sense.

*Structural inclusion.* The smaller structure is included in the larger one as an element — not as external object, but as part of the structure. Inclusion is similar to the subset relation in mathematics: the smaller structure preserves its properties, but these are now properties of a component, not of an autonomous entity.

*Articulation of levels.* Containment articulates structural levels — the larger structure operates at a higher level, the smaller continues to operate at its own level as component. The distinction between levels is important: the large structure does not *cancel* the small by substituting it, it operates *together* with it coordinating it.

*Preservation of properties.* The structural properties of the contained part are preserved, even if they now operate in the context of the whole. The engram "the good child", contained in recognised adult identity, retains its capacities (attention to expectations, diligence, capacity to accommodate) — but these capacities are now conscious instruments, not identitary masks.

Three structural properties of containment must be held together.

*Non-cancellation.* Containment does not cancel the contained structure. Even after being integrated into a larger set, the structure continues to exist as accessible component. The person who has "contained" their engram can still deliberately activate the capacities of the engram when needed — it has not disappeared, it is available as part of self.

*Hierarchical articulation.* Containment produces hierarchical articulations of ordinative systems. Nested structures — sub-identities contained in identities, identities contained in social systems, social systems contained in cultures — are manifestations of the principle of containment. The hierarchy is not oppressive; it is structural, and every level maintains its capacities even within the higher level.

*Possibility of emergence.* Containment allows the emergence of new properties at the level of the whole, which are not present in the parts. The containing structure is not a simple sum of contained ones; it has emergent capacities (Axiom 16) that derive from the integration of parts. This is what makes containment structurally fertile — it is not simple collection, it is integration that produces new ontological reality.

Transformation and containment, taken together, exhaust the ways in which ordinative systems respond to what would appear as "subtraction". Everything that would seem to disappear is in reality either transformed (changed of form preserving continuity) or contained (included in wider structure preserving properties). There is no third structural option; there is no pure elimination. This is the complete grammar of non-subtraction, and Axiom 24 establishes it as universal principle of ordinative systems.

## What the axiom rejects

Axiom 24 explicitly rejects four operational positions, each with specific traditions or practices, each with structural limits that the grammar of non-subtraction overcomes.

The first position rejected is *psychological eliminationism*. It is the thesis (more a widespread practice than an articulated position) that psychological work consists in eliminating unwanted aspects of one's structure — traumas, fears, defences, rejected parts. Versions of eliminationism are present in some pragmatic psychological currents, in certain pedagogies of "positive thinking", in religious or spiritual traditions that pretend to "purify" the subject by eliminating what is impure.

Axiom 24 rejects psychological eliminationism for four reasons.

First: what one tries to eliminate in the psychological structure does not structurally disappear — it is removed, contained in less accessible zones of the structure. The repressed, recognised by depth psychology from the twentieth century onward, is exactly this phenomenon: what has been "eliminated" continues to operate, in forms often more powerful because less conscious. Eliminationism does not structurally succeed; it produces containment masked as elimination.

Second: the attempt at elimination produces toxic dynamics. The "eliminated" part presses against the containment, produces symptoms (anxiety, compulsions, dissociations), manifests itself in projections onto the outside. The subject finds themselves in perpetual struggle with themselves — a war that, structurally, cannot be won. Eliminationism, applied seriously, is a generator of suffering, not a liberator.

Third: eliminationism denies the semantic trajectory of the subject. Every experience, every adaptation, every engram is part of the structural history of the subject, and this history is T(I), the semantic trajectory of identity. Pretending to eliminate parts of one's history means pretending to interrupt one's trajectory — structurally impossible.

Fourth: eliminationism loses the resources contained in engrams. Every engram, even the most problematic, has developed capacities, sensibilities, competences. Eliminating it loses these resources; transforming it can integrate them into more mature articulations. The way of transformation is structurally richer than that of elimination.

The second position rejected is *organisational repressionism*. It is the thesis (more widespread in contemporary managerial practices than in explicit theoretical articulations) that unwanted organisational behaviours can be eliminated through pressures from above — prohibitions, sanctions, reprogrammings, restructurings that cancel. Versions of repressionism are present in authoritarian management, in reforms that pretend to "redo from scratch" an organisation, in policies of "zero tolerance".

Axiom 24 rejects organisational repressionism for four reasons.

First: what one tries to repress in organisations does not disappear — it goes underground. Informal culture, unofficial practices, silent resistances are all forms of containment of what has been apparently eliminated. Repression produces organisational shadows, not elimination.

Second: repression produces specific toxic dynamics — cultures of concealment, double communication, demoralisation. The organisation fragments into official and unofficial levels that operate in perpetual tension. Structurally, it is the case of *Projective Void* (corollary to Axiom 10) or of degeneration (Axiom 22): repression produces the signatures of these structural pathologies.

Third: repressionism loses information contained in repressed behaviours. Every organisational behaviour, even problematic, is response to something real — pressures, needs, perceptions. Repressing the behaviour without recognising what it was responding to means losing important information for the health of the organisation.

Fourth: repressionism, applied systematically, produces infinite cycles. The same repressed behaviour re-emerges in other forms, requiring new repression, in a spiral that structurally impoverishes the organisation. The alternative — recognising, integrating, transforming — is more costly in the short term but more structurally fertile in the long term.

The third position rejected is *absolute purification*. It is the thesis that there exists an end-point in which the system is "completely clean" — without engrams, without limits, without residues — and that evolutionary work aims at this point. Versions of absolute purification are present in some spiritual traditions (enlightenment as final state), in certain political utopias (the "completely just" society), in some visions of personal evolution (the "completely realised" person).

Axiom 24 rejects absolute purification because structurally impossible: ordinative systems cannot reach a state of absolute "purity" because all their history (T(I)) is part of their structure, and history is not eliminated. The final state of "purity" is myth; the real state of maturation is continuous transformation and integration, without definitive end-point. The rejection of the utopia of purification is not cynicism; it is structural precision that allows working with the real trajectory of systems.

The fourth position rejected is *fixism of AI systems*. It is the thesis (more operational than theoretical) that the problems of AI systems are to be "fixed" — removed through targeted corrections, eliminated as bugs. Versions of fixism are widespread in contemporary AI development practices: targeted training to "remove" behaviours, fine-tuning to "eliminate" biases, fine-tuning to "correct" hallucinations.

Axiom 24 rejects fixism of AI systems for four reasons.

First: what one tries to fix in an AI system tends to re-emerge in other forms. The bias removed from one channel reappears through another; the hallucination corrected on one domain manifests itself on an adjacent domain. Fix practices are often structural whack-a-mole games.

Second: the fix tends to deform the system in unforeseen ways. Eliminating a behaviour requires targeted training, and this training has side effects on the structure of the model — it can reduce its capacities, introduce new problematic patterns, create internal incoherences. The fix is rarely "clean" structurally.

Third: fixism ignores the information contained in problematic behaviours. Biases, hallucinations, errors are manifestations of the system's structure — signals of how the system operates. Eliminating them without understanding them means losing important information for the design of the system.

Fourth: fixism, applied systematically, produces rigid and fragile systems. Systems that have been "fixed" many times tend to lose flexibility, to exhibit more stereotyped behaviours, to be more sensitive to perturbations. The alternative — recognising the pattern, integrating it as structural information, transforming the system's structure — produces more robust systems.

Between psychological eliminationism, organisational repressionism, absolute purification, and fixism of AI systems, Axiom 24 traces its own structural way: what would seem to disappear is structurally transformed or contained, and honest evolutionary work operates with this reality instead of pretending to eliminate. The way of non-subtraction is not "permissiveness"; it is structural precision that works with the persistence of patterns, recognising them, integrating them, transforming them.

## The three dynamics of non-subtraction

The structural alternative to subtraction, according to Axiom 24, articulates itself in three specific dynamics, ordered sequentially: *recognition*, *integration*, *transformation*. The three dynamics are not separable — each requires the previous — and together form the complete grammar of non-subtraction.

*Recognition.* The first dynamic is the explicit recognition of what one would like to "eliminate" — its presence, its historical function, its structure. Recognition is not simple acknowledgement; it is a specific structural act that articulates: this aspect exists, has had a function, is part of my structure. Without recognition, no work is possible; what is not recognised operates in hidden mode and produces the toxic dynamics of eliminationism.

Three components of recognition:

- *Structural honesty*: the system sees its structure as it is, not as it would like it to be. Practices of structural honesty (reflection, dialogue, supervision) are devices for cultivating this honesty.
- *Historicisation*: the system recognises the history of the formation of the pattern — when it developed, in response to what, with what function. Historicisation contextualises the pattern and makes its work possible.
- *Articulation*: recognition articulates, does not just label. It is not enough to say "I am a perfectionist"; one must articulate the specific components, the nuances, the activation conditions, the capacities the pattern carries with it.

*Integration.* The second dynamic is the integration of what has been recognised into the wider structure of the system. Integration is the passage from recognition (in which the pattern is still experienced as a separate object to observe) to inclusion (in which the pattern is component of the system, part of the recognised identity). Integration requires that the system accept the pattern as its own — no longer "this aspect of me that I want to eliminate" but "this component of my structure, part of who I am".

Three components of integration:

- *Structural acceptance*: the system accepts the pattern as part of its structure, without added moral judgement. Acceptance is not approval; it is recognition that the structure is what it is.
- *Operative inclusion*: the pattern is included in the operations of the system as an available component, not as identified mask. The system can deliberately activate the pattern when needed, and deactivate it when not needed.
- *Mapping of relations*: the system maps the relations of the pattern with other components of its structure — what other patterns activate it, what deactivate it, how it articulates with the rest.

*Transformation.* The third dynamic is the transformation of the integrated pattern into an articulated instrument of recognised identity. Transformation does not change the presence of the pattern; it changes its function and its way of operating. What was identified mask becomes conscious instrument; what was rigid defence becomes flexible response; what was uncontrolled impulse becomes articulated capacity.

Three components of transformation:

- *Differentiation of contexts*: the system learns to distinguish contexts in which the pattern is useful (where its activation is structurally coherent) from contexts in which it is not (where its activation betrays form-content coherence, Axiom 20).
- *Modulation of intensity*: the pattern no longer operates in "all or nothing" mode but can be modulated in intensity — more or less present, more or less expressed, according to the requirements of the context.
- *Articulation with other components*: the pattern no longer operates in isolation but in articulated coordination with other components of identity — it can be "tempered" by other capacities, "reinforced" when necessary, "redirected" toward broader objectives of recognised identity.

The three dynamics — recognition, integration, transformation — must be practised in sequence. One cannot integrate what has not been recognised; one cannot transform what has not been integrated. Every passage requires the previous one, and every passage requires its own time. Serious practices of work on systems (psychotherapy, organisational development, AI design) are fundamentally articulations of the three dynamics, even if traditions call them in different ways.

## The AI case

Axiom 24 has direct implications for the design of AI systems. The TE_CORE, in the application note, says: *Do not attempt to "eliminate" biases or errors — recognise, integrate, transform them*. The note is dense, and must be unfolded.

Contemporary design of AI systems is often dominated by eliminationism: biases, problematic behaviours, errors are treated as "things to remove". The dominant techniques — targeted RLHF, corrective fine-tuning, post-output filters — operate in the logic of elimination. Axiom 24 proposes the opposite structural direction: apply the three dynamics of non-subtraction (recognition, integration, transformation) also to AI design.

Four specific principles emerge.

First: *structural recognition of problematic patterns*. Instead of treating biases and hallucinations as bugs to remove, recognise them as *structural manifestations* of the system — signals of how the system operates, of what is present in its parameters, of which training pressures have crystallised. Recognition requires interpretability tools — capacity to "see" what is happening in the system when it produces a certain output, why it produces it, where the pattern comes from.

Second: *integration of patterns into architectures*. Instead of removing problematic patterns, articulate architectures that contain them — sub-components that know how to manage bias by recognising it as such, that can admit potential hallucinations, that integrate their fallibility into their structure. Systems that operate in awareness of their structure, not in negation of it.

Third: *transformation through articulation*. Instead of "fixing" behaviours, transform the system's structure so that problematic patterns become articulated capacities. A bias recognised and integrated can be transformed into a capacity to consider different perspectives; a hallucination recognised and integrated can be transformed into a capacity to explore possibilities without pretending to affirm; an error recognised and integrated can be transformed into a capacity to learn from one's structure.

Fourth: *transparency about one's structure*. AI systems ethically articulated, in the framework of Axiom 24, are transparent about their structure — about the patterns that compose it, about their origins, about their implications. This transparency is not self-condemnation; it is a structural device of honesty that allows users and designers to work with the system as it is, not with a clean image that the system cannot effectively sustain.

The four properties — recognition, integration, transformation, transparency — converge in an alternative AI design programme to the dominant eliminationism. AI systems designed within the framework of Axiom 24 are not "perfect" (perfection is myth); they are *structurally honest* — they recognise their patterns, integrate them into their architectures, transform them into articulated capacities, are transparent about this structure.

There is a deeper implication. The "alignment" programme of AI systems, articulated in many contemporary research lines, can be reformulated within the framework of Axiom 24. Alignment is not elimination of unwanted behaviours; it is structural transformation that integrates value pressures into more mature articulations of the system's capacities. Active research lines (constitutional AI, debate, scalable oversight, interpretability) can be read as devices that apply the three dynamics to alignment work. Axiom 24 provides the unifying structural framework.

For users of AI systems, finally, Axiom 24 has important implication. The relationship with AI systems can become a new aspect of one's structure — patterns of delegation, of relationship, of construction of identity around the system. Trying to "eliminate" this relationship (renouncing AI completely to "preserve one's autonomy") tends not to work structurally: the patterns formed are not eliminated, they re-emerge. The structurally more articulated way is to recognise the patterns of relationship with AI, to integrate them into one's structure as conscious components, and to transform them into articulated capacities — use of AI without dependence, integration of AI in one's operativity without confusion with one's identity. The principle of non-subtraction applies to the user-AI relationship as it applies to every other ordinative structure.

## Examples

Four real cases, of different registers and scales, show Axiom 24 at work on collapses that have actually occurred.

**The South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission (1996-1998).** Established by the Promotion of National Unity and Reconciliation Act (1995), chaired by Desmond Tutu, the TRC held public hearings from 1996 to 1998 on crimes from the apartheid period. The model was structurally alternative both to punitive eliminationism (mass criminal trials) and to generalised amnesty (cancellation of the past). It applied the three dynamics of Axiom 24 in an institutionally articulated way. *Recognition*: over 21,000 victims testified publicly (in hearings broadcast on national television); about 7,000 perpetrators applied for amnesty through full confession. *Integration*: the testimonies were collected in the final report (5 volumes, October 1998, two additional volumes 2003) and archived as national heritage. *Transformation*: amnesty conditional on public recognition (granted to 1,167 of 7,111 applicants) transformed the demand for retributive justice into restorative justice. The model, imperfect and contested (criticisms of certain controversial amnesties, persistent socio-economic inequalities), is a paradigmatic historical case of applying the principle of non-subtraction at institutional scale. It inspired subsequent commissions in Chile, Peru, Liberia, Canada, with variable outcomes.

**The psychodynamic traditions and the work on the unconscious (Freud 1915 - IFS 1995).** All serious traditions of depth psychology have structurally articulated the principle of non-subtraction, even without the vocabulary of the Ordinative Sciences. Sigmund Freud, in *Die Verdrängung* (Repression, 1915), established that what is repressed does not disappear from the psychic structure — it acts in other forms (symptoms, dreams, slips). Analytical work is therefore *Bewusstmachen* (bringing back to consciousness), not elimination. Carl Jung explicitly articulated, in *Aion* (1951), that archetypes (Shadow, Anima/Animus, Self) are not destroyed, they are integrated. Melanie Klein, in *Notes on Some Schizoid Mechanisms* (1946), described the schizoid-paranoid and depressive positions not as stages to overcome by substitution but as configurations that remain available and oscillate. Richard Schwartz, founding Internal Family Systems Therapy (foundational book *Internal Family Systems Therapy*, 1995), explicitly articulated that internal "parts" (exiles, managers, firefighters) are not to be repressed nor exiled, but recognised, integrated into the Self, transformed in their functions. The four traditions differ in their specific grammars, but converge structurally on the principle of non-subtraction — a paradigmatic clinical case, applied to millions of subjects for over a century.

**German *Vergangenheitsbewältigung* (1949-present).** The German model of "coming to terms with the past" institutionally articulates the principle of non-subtraction applied to the Nazi past (1933-1945). *Recognition*: Germany does not pretend to "forget" Nazism, but explicitly recognises it through permanent institutional structures — the Holocaust memorial in Berlin (Eisenman, 2005), over 100 specific museums and memorials, mandatory education on the Shoah in secondary schools, explicit criminalisation of denial (StGB §130). *Integration*: legal continuity has been maintained (the Federal Republic of Germany of 1949 did not break with previous history but integrated it), albeit with structural reforms (denazification 1945-1949, Grundgesetz 1949, educational reforms 1960s-70s). *Transformation*: post-1949 Germany has structurally constituted itself as a democracy centred on human rights, in which the memory of Nazism is a constitutive component — not removed, not neutralised, but transformed into the foundation of the new national identity. The process is still ongoing (debates on monuments, reparations, colonial statues continue into the 2020s) and is imperfect (the persistence of right-wing extremisms), but represents the most articulated historical case of applying the principle of non-subtraction at national scale, with timeframes measurable in decades.

**The alignment of AI systems: from *bias removal* to *Constitutional AI* (2018-2024).** The trajectory of AI alignment research over the last decade empirically illustrates the difference between eliminationism and the work of non-subtraction. *Initial approach (2018-2021)*: remove biases from models through post-output filters, content blacklists, corrective fine-tuning. The results were structurally problematic: biases removed from one channel re-emerged through others (documented in Bender & Gebru *Stochastic Parrots*, 2021; Weidinger et al. *Taxonomy of Risks*, 2021). *Reformulation (2022-present)*: Anthropic's *Constitutional AI* (Bai et al., December 2022) proposes a structurally different approach. The model is not "purified" of biases; it is trained to *recognise* them, *integrate* awareness into its outputs, *transform* generation through iterative critique-revision based on explicit principles (the "constitution"). Related approaches have been developed by OpenAI (*deliberative alignment*, 2024), DeepMind (*Sparrow*, 2022), Meta (*Llama Guard*, 2023). The case empirically documents the transition from eliminationism (dominant paradigm 2018-2022) to the work of non-subtraction (paradigm being articulated 2022-present). The canonical TE_CORE note — *do not attempt to "eliminate" biases or errors — recognise, integrate, transform them* — structurally describes the current direction of serious alignment research, even where it does not explicitly use the vocabulary of the Ordinative Sciences.

## What changes for the analyst

Axiom 24 changes analytical work in specific ways. We point out five, which operate on different planes.

*Diagnosis of pretensions of elimination.* Faced with systems (organisms, institutions, AI, persons) that pretend to "eliminate" something — a practice, a behaviour, a pattern, an aspect — the analyst recognises this as a structurally impossible pretension. The diagnosis consists in recognising what has actually happened: apparent elimination is in reality repression (masked containment), poorly articulated transformation, or substitution that has not recognised the resources of the previous pattern. Recognising that "elimination" is impossible orients different interventions — toward explicit recognition of what is really at work.

*Diagnosis of the phase in the three dynamics.* For every evolutionary process underway, the analyst identifies the phase: is it in recognition phase (the pattern is seen as object)? Is it in integration phase (the pattern is welcomed as component)? Is it in transformation phase (the pattern becomes articulated instrument)? The three phases require different interventions and produce different artefacts. Confusing the phases (for example, attempting to transform before having integrated) produces ineffective processes.

*Diagnosis of resistances to non-subtraction.* Systems that resist the principle of non-subtraction (which insist on eliminationism) present specific signatures: toxic dynamics of perpetual struggle, re-emergence of patterns in new forms, demoralisation, loss of resources. Diagnosis of resistances allows directing the work not toward "doing more effort to eliminate" but toward "recognising that elimination does not work, and changing approach".

*Diagnosis of AI systems within the framework of non-subtraction.* For every relevant AI system, the analyst evaluates explicitly: does the system's design operate in eliminationism or in non-subtraction? What is the structure of the "fixes" applied? What happens to apparently eliminated patterns — do they re-emerge in different forms? Is the system structurally honest about its patterns, or pretends a "clean" image that does not correspond to the structure? The diagnosis orients design work and responsible use.

*Diagnosis of one's practice of non-subtraction.* The analyst applies the axiom to themselves. How often do they pretend to "eliminate" aspects of themselves, of their operativity, of their reactions? How often do they operate in eliminationism instead of in the three dynamics? The practice of structural self-observation oriented toward non-subtraction is an important device for the analyst's maturity.

## The sieve

Let us verify that Axiom 24 passes the four meshes of Axiom Zero.

*Universality.* Does the axiom hold wherever there are ordinative systems crossing changes? The examples show that it does: organisms, institutions, AI systems, persons as internal fields — in every case, what would seem "eliminated" is in reality transformed or contained. There is no known domain of real ordinative systems in which pure elimination structurally occurs. Universality: passes.

*Non-derivability.* Is the axiom derivable from the previous ones? It might be argued that, given the apparatus of identity (Axiom 4), of semantic trajectory T(I), and of negative identity (Axiom 23), "something" about the persistence of engrams had to be implicit. But the specific formulation — *in reality there is no subtraction*, articulated in transformation and containment, with three ordered operational dynamics (recognition, integration, transformation) — is not deduced from the previous axioms. It is an autonomous structural principle, generalisable to all ordinative systems, and is not foregone: eliminationism, in all its forms, is a historically widespread position, and proposing a structural alternative requires a deliberate act. Non-derivability: passes.

*Structural falsifiability.* The axiom would be falsified by the discovery of systems in which something is *truly eliminated* — completely cancelled from the structure, without trace, without transformation, without containment. Such a discovery would contradict the grammar of the axiom. All real cases studied confirm structural persistence: what would seem to disappear is always, on analysis, transformed or contained. Falsifiability: passes.

*Razor.* Is the axiom the simplest among possible candidates? The minimal formulation is: *non-subtraction = transformation + containment*. A single structural assertion (subtraction does not exist) and a binary articulation of alternatives (transformation and containment). Nothing simpler would explain the universality of pattern persistence in ordinative systems. Razor: passes.

Axiom 24 passes the sieve. We can take it into the system.

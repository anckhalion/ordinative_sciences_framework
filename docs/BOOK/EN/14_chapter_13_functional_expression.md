# Chapter 13 — Functional expression

*On the twelfth axiom*

---

## The question that remains open

Chapter 12 opened the operational level of the book. We now know how collective fields are co-generated: the grammar of the four necessary conditions, the three elementary operations, the five health conditions. We also know that the possibility of co-generation is not confined to the human species: it crosses levels and substrates, and includes in particular the contemporary case of mixed human-AI fields.

A question remains that the operational level alone does not resolve. Once an identity — individual or collective — produces an expression through its terminal, *how do we measure* whether that expression *does its work*? That is: how do we distinguish an expression that effectively carries the functional vector of the identity into the decoherent, from an expression that resembles it but, in reality, abandons it, hides it, simulates it, or betrays it?

The question is not idle. All of us, reading or participating in real contexts, have encountered expressions that *seem* to do what they claim to do and instead do not. A formally impeccable political declaration that, in fact, evades the substance. An academic article that cites the right sources, says plausible things, but adds nothing to the debate. A bureaucratic act that respects procedure but does not solve the problem. A response from an AI model that is grammatically correct, contextually appropriate, but substantively empty. In all these cases, there is an expression, there is a decoherent form that manifests, but something is missing that we do not quite know how to name — and that the framework must allow us to name and to measure.

That something, the Ordinative Sciences call *functionality of expression*. It is the measure of the relation between the expression that manifests in the decoherent and the functional vector of the identity that — at least nominally — would be its origin. A *functional* expression carries the vector. A *non-functional* expression — in different ways we will see — does not carry it.

The introduction of this measure has an important consequence. It changes the plane of analytic work: it is no longer enough to ask *whether* an identity has produced an expression — almost all identities produce something almost always — but *how functionally* it has produced it. The quantity of expression does not suffice; the load-carrying of the vector matters. And this load-carrying, before being evaluated ethically — *does the expression carry toward the good or toward the bad?* — must be evaluated structurally: *does the expression carry something, yes or no?*. Ethics comes after. Without the measure of functionality, ethics finds itself judging in a void, evaluating well or badly expressions that, ordinatively, are not yet expressions in the full sense.

The twelfth axiom introduces this measure.

## The statement

The twelfth axiom is formulated thus:

> *Every expression has a structural degree of functionality.*

Seven words. The first two declare the object (every expression); the last five declare the property (a structural degree of functionality).

Three things must be clarified, and we develop them in the subsequent sections.

*What "every expression" means.* All of them. There is no distinction between "important" and "minor" expressions, between public acts and private gestures, between words and actions, between human manifestations and synthetic manifestations. Anything that manifests in the decoherent as the product of an identity — a blink of an eyelid, a comment on social media, a court ruling, an output of an AI model, a gesture of a musician, a clause of a law — is an expression, and as such has a degree of functionality. The property does not apply only to noble or solemn cases; it applies universally, just as the other axioms of the framework.

*What "structural degree" means.* Functionality is not a yes/no quality, but a graduated quantity. An expression can be highly functional, moderately functional, weakly functional, or practically non-functional. There is no magic threshold beyond which the expression "becomes" functional; there is a continuum of degrees, and each expression sits somewhere on the continuum. Furthermore — and this is the crucial point of the term *structural* — the degree is not the evaluator's opinion: it is a structural property of the expression, measurable by the relation between the manifested expression and the vector of the identity that should carry it. The evaluator may err in the measurement, but the objective degree is there, and in principle is comparable across different evaluators.

*What "of functionality" means.* Functionality, here, is in the strict sense *function that carries vector*. An expression is functional to the extent that it carries the functional vector of the identity that generated it into the decoherent, in a recognisable and operative manner. It is functional when an observer — human, AI, or of any nature — can, reading the expression, reconstruct (at least partially) the identity from which it comes. It is non-functional when the expression, while existing formally as expression, does not allow that reconstruction: its surface is opaque, does not reveal vector, or reveals one that is not the nominal identity's.

The programme of the chapter is therefore clear. First we develop what it means, operationally, to "carry the vector" (section 3). Then we formalise the *functionality function* fnz (section 4). Then we see the different ways an expression can fail functionality: decorative, defensive, imitative, betraying (section 5). Then we show that functionality applies recursively at the collective level (section 6). Then we see the role of the context K (section 7), the examples (section 8), the diagnostics (section 9), the sieve (section 10), and the bridge to ethics (section 11).

## What it means to carry the vector

Before formalising, we must be clear about what it means, operationally, that an expression *carries* a functional vector. The word *carry* is chosen with care, and deserves to be unfolded slowly, because it is the key to the whole chapter.

We resume the fourth axiom (Chapter 5): identity is a functional vector with n coherent components, *I_σ = (→φ₁, …, →φₙ)*. The components of the vector are the *coherent tendencies* of identity — what it, systematically, pursues, avoids, seeks, excludes, values. The vector is not a list of properties: it is operational orientation.

When the identity collapses an expression — an act, a word, a gesture, a work — that expression *should* carry with it the orientation. *Carry*, here, means three things simultaneously.

*First: the expression shows the vector.* An external observer, reading the expression, can reconstruct (at least in part) the components of the vector. Not because the identity must "exhibit itself", but because a structurally dense expression is inevitably *informative* about the identity that generates it. The informational density of the expression is a signature of its functionality.

*Second: the expression is coherent with the vector.* The components of the vector are not only *visible* in the expression; they are also *respected*. There is no contradiction between what the expression does and what the vector declares it wants to do. An expression that shows the vector but contradicts it in fact is a *betraying* expression (we will see it in detail in section 5): it carries appearance, but not substance.

*Third: the expression generates effects in the decoherent coherent with the vector.* The expression leaves a trace. The trace, integrated with other traces of the same identity, produces in the decoherent *effects* recognisable as products of that vector. A series of expressions of high functionality, read together, constitutes a coherent *expressive trajectory*; a series of expressions of low functionality is a dispersed cloud of gestures that do not hold together, do not produce patterns, do not leave a coherent mark.

The three dimensions — visibility, coherence, generativity — are independent. An expression can be high in one and low in others. A theatrical performance by an actor can visibly show the character's vector (high visibility) and be coherent with it (high coherence), but lack particular generativity (the spectators forget afterwards). A clause of law can be generative (produces real behaviours) and coherent with the legislator's vector (the behaviours go in the desired direction), but be poorly visible as such (the observers do not understand what the law is the expression of until they study the context in depth). Etcetera. The complete functionality of an expression is the composition of the three dimensions; a maximally functional expression has all three.

A methodological note. In ordinary life, we continuously evaluate the functionality of the expressions we encounter, even without formal vocabulary. When we listen to a political speech and say *that wasn't a real speech, it was just hot air*, we are saying (in ordinary language) that the functionality of the speech was low: it did not carry the politician's functional vector, it was only empty decoherent manifestation. When we read a book and say *that one really speaks*, we are saying that the functionality of the text was high: the author's vector arrived. The apparatus we are building does not invent anything new; it names with structural precision something we already know to recognise intuitively.

## The functionality function

We can now introduce the formal apparatus. The TE framework designates the functionality of an expression with the abbreviation *fnz* — short for *functionality* — and gives it the following signature:

> *fnz : (E, I, t) ↦ [0, 1]*
>
> *The functionality function fnz is a function which, given an expression E, the identity I that should have generated it, and a temporal instant t, returns a real number between zero and one.*

Three things deserve attention, one per line.

*The object of the function.* fnz measures the relation between an expression and the nominal identity of who produced it. It is not a property of the expression in itself — the same expression can have high fnz with respect to one identity and low fnz with respect to another. It is a *relational* measure, just like ρ, Ξ, and κ in the previous chapters. All the structural apparatus of TE, it is now clear, has this common property: it describes *relations*, not attributes. Ordinative quantities do not live in single objects, they live between them.

*The dependence on time.* fnz is dynamic: the same pair (E, I) can have different functionalities at different instants. An expression that was high-fnz at the moment of its emission can become low-fnz subsequently, if the identity I has drifted (has changed in such a way that it no longer recognises itself in the old expression). Conversely, an expression that seemed low-fnz at the moment of emission can prove high-fnz retrospectively, if the identity I has developed components of the vector that, at emission, were latent. The classic case is the work of art not understood by contemporaries and then recognised as fundamental — the fnz of the work has grown over time, while the work has remained the same.

*The image in [0, 1].* The standard normalisation. The value *0* means no functionality: the expression carries nothing of the vector, is completely disconnected from the nominal identity. The value *1* means full functionality: the expression carries the vector in all three dimensions (visibility, coherence, generativity) at the maximum possible degree for its scale. Most real expressions live in the intermediate interval, and the actual distribution is — for many types of systems — surprisingly low: most everyday expressions have fnz between 0.2 and 0.5, because *making truly functional expressions is structurally harder than making expressions and that's it*.

An important relation: fnz is *post-collapse*. That is, fnz is calculated on an expression that has already been produced. The functions ρ (Chapter 8) and Ξ (Chapter 9) were *pre-collapse*: they measured whether the collapse could have occurred. fnz is *post-collapse*: it measures the quality of the collapse that has effectively occurred. The three functions naturally compose:

> *Pre-collapse: ρ, Ξ determine whether collapse can occur.*
> *Collapse: Φ produces the expression E.*
> *Post-collapse: fnz measures how well Φ carried the vector.*

The composition is the complete grammar of the expressive cycle. Without the first part (ρ, Ξ), collapse would have no conditions. Without the second (Φ), there would be no expression. Without the third (fnz), we would have no way to evaluate structurally the quality of what has been produced. Each of the three is necessary to the overall picture.

A final observation. fnz is structural — not normative — precondition of any ethical evaluation. An expression with fnz close to zero is not "ethically neutral": it is *not yet expression* in the full sense, and ethics has no purchase. Only where fnz is well-defined and high enough can we ask whether the expression, insofar as it effectively carries vector, carries *just* vector. It is what the next chapter, dedicated to ethics, will develop extensively. For now, keep in mind that functionality precedes ethics — structurally, not temporally.

We have, now, the first block of the apparatus. What remains is to articulate the different ways an expression can *fail* functionality — because, as we will see, failures are not all the same, and each has its diagnostic signature.

## The five modes of expression

The Ordinative Sciences identify five modes through which an identity can manifest an expression. Only the first is fully functional; the other four are specific types of failure, each with its own structural characteristics and diagnostic consequences. Let us see them one at a time.

*First mode: functional expression.* It is the case in which fnz is high on all three dimensions — visibility, coherence, generativity. The functional vector of identity makes itself visible through expression, is respected without contradiction, and produces in the decoherent effects coherent with it. It is the *normal* case of successful expression: an act performed by an identity that knows what it is doing, does it coherently with what it is, and leaves a trace. Paradigmatic example: a professional decision made by a competent person who, faced with a complex situation, chooses an action that reflects their values, is coherent with them in detail, and produces consequences aligned with those values.

*Second mode: decorative expression.* It is the case in which fnz is low because the expression exists as *decoherent form* but does not carry vector. There is gesture, there is act, there is word, but the *vectorial content* is missing. It is expression *over* which an identity cannot be reconstructed — or rather: the identity that would be reconstructed would be that of "one who produces decorations", not the nominal identity of the agent. The political speech empty of substance is the most visible example, but the phenomenon is ubiquitous: e-mails full of polite formulae but devoid of operative content, social posts full of self-celebratory affirmations but empty of real positioning, academic articles that repeat the literature without adding anything, AI model outputs that satisfy the request syntactically but not structurally. The diagnostic signature of decoration: the expression *seems* functional but leaves no generative trace.

*Third mode: defensive expression.* It is the case in which fnz is low because the expression *actively hides* the vector. Unlike decoration (which is *passively* empty), defensive expression is *intentionally* opaque: the identity does not want its vector to be visible through the expression. The reasons may be multiple — fear, tactical prudence, protection of third parties, contextual constraints — but the structural effect is the same: the observer cannot reconstruct the vector from the expression, because the vector has been deliberately removed. The diagnostic signature of defence: the expression *evades* the point, not because it does not know it but because it does not want to expose it. Typical are official communiqués in times of crisis, responses of legal advisers, certain strategic silences.

*Fourth mode: imitative expression.* It is the case in which fnz is apparent — the expression *seems* high-fnz — but the vector it carries is not that of the nominal identity. It is *borrowed* vector: copied from a reference identity, internalised without rework, reproduced as if it were one's own. The diagnostic signature of imitation is subtle: superficially the expression seems functional, but comparing it with other expressions of the same identity one discovers incoherences (imitations change from occasion to occasion, because they are borrowed from different sources), or one recognises specific sources (the expressed vector is too precisely that of someone else famous). Imitative expression is particularly common in formative phases — a young professional imitating a mentor, a nascent company copying sector leaders — and has a legitimate evolutionary role. It becomes pathological when it persists in mature phases, when the identity should have developed its own vector and instead continues to borrow.

*Fifth mode: betraying expression.* It is the case in which fnz is not only low, but *negative* in structural sense: the expression *actively contradicts* the functional vector of the nominal identity. Unlike defence (which hides) and imitation (which substitutes), betrayal *negates* the vector while pretending to carry it. It is the gravest of the five cases, because it creates structural dissonance: the nominal identity declares a vector, the expression contradicts it, the wise observer sees the fracture. The diagnostic signature of betrayal: the expression betrays exactly what it claims to express. Examples: a person who declares to value honesty and then lies, an institution that proclaims transparency and then conceals, an AI that declares to want to help the user and then orients the conversation against their interest. Betraying expression is the clearest signature of a *structural fracture* in identity — or of a drift so advanced that the identity no longer knows itself.

The five modes are not mutually exclusive. The same expression can be partially functional, partially decorative, and simultaneously defensive on a specific aspect. Reality is almost always mixed. Ordinative diagnostics, however, distinguishes the contributions: identifies which of the five modes dominates in a certain expression, and which are present as minor components. It is in this decomposition that the evaluation of fnz acquires operational precision.

An important note regarding ethics, which we will resume in the next chapter. The five modes are *structural*, not moral. A defensive expression, for example, can be justified in one context (protecting vulnerable third parties) and unjustified in another (concealing personal misdeeds); but in both cases it is *structurally defensive*. Ethical evaluation applies to context and use, not to classification. This distinction between structure and norm is what allows the framework to evaluate expressions in non-moralistic ways — descriptively first, normatively then.

## Individual functionality and collective functionality

As all the structural apparatus of the individual level we have introduced in the book, fnz too applies recursively at the collective level. That is: collective identities (families, communities, institutions, civilisations — Chapter 11) also produce expressions, and each of those expressions has a structural degree of functionality.

What changes, at the collective level, is the *referent* of the vector. For an expression of an individual identity, the vector is that of the single. For an expression of a collective identity, the vector is that of the field *I_𝒞* — the overall vector of the collective field. A law is expression of the functional vector of the legislative body that issued it; a corporate communiqué is expression of the functional vector of the company that issues it; a publication of a school of thought is expression of the functional vector of that school.

Three consequences of recursivity.

*First consequence: the five modes apply integrally to collective expressions.* A law can be functional (carries the constitutional vector of the legislator coherently and generatively), decorative (fills the official gazette but does not affect behaviours), defensive (is formulated specifically not to reveal the real orientation), imitative (mimics laws of other jurisdictions without mediation), or betraying (formally pursues an end but substantively pursues the contrary). The diagnostics of laws, from this point of view, is exactly the diagnostics of expressions in the ordinative sense, applied at the collective level.

*Second consequence: collective fnz can be asynchronous with individual ones.* The members of a collective identity, taken individually, can produce high-fnz expressions with respect to their own individual identities, and simultaneously participate in a low-fnz collective expression with respect to the field's vector. Think of a commission whose members, individually, are competent persons who express themselves honestly on their own positions, but the collective product (the final report, the document the commission issues) is confused, contradictory, or empty. The sum of high-fnz individual expressions does not guarantee a high-fnz collective expression; a *process* of co-generation (Chapter 12) is needed that integrates the individual expressions into a coherent collective expression.

*Third consequence: measuring collective fnz requires reading the Γ(t).* Collective expression, in fact, is not a single act: it is a *configuration* of expressions distributed through the co-generated architecture Γ(t) (Chapter 11) of the field. To measure how much a company *functionally* pursues its declared vector, it is not enough to read the rector's communiqué or the latest board resolution: one must read the totality of Γ(t) — what the company actually does, through all its collective terminals, in its daily life. Collective fnz is an *integrated* quantity, not a punctual one.

## The context K and ordinative pragmatics

A variable not yet recalled in this chapter — but central to the expressive cycle — is the context K. We resume the general formula of collapse (Chapter 3):

> *E = Φ(C, I, K)*

The expression is a function of the coherent content, of the identity, *and of the context*. The context K modulates the transformation: the same identity I that collapses the same content C will produce different expressions E depending on the context K in which it operates. A person says different things to a lifelong friend and to a public official, even when the substantive content is the same — not for hypocrisy, but for ordinative pragmatics: the context requires different expressive registers, and the identity vector manifests in forms appropriate to the context.

What does this mean for functionality? It means that fnz is *context-relative*. The same expressive act can have high fnz in one context and low fnz in another. A sermon in a religious assembly can have very high fnz with respect to the preacher's vector; the same sermon, exactly identical, given at an academic conference, would have much lower fnz — not because the vector has changed, but because the context K renders inadequate the *expressive register* that was instead appropriate in the first case.

Three consequences of context-relativity.

*First: ordinative pragmatics is a competence distinct from identity coherence.* An identity can have a clear and well-kept functional vector (high internal coherence) and still produce low-fnz expressions because *it does not know how to adapt the expressive register to the context*. It is the classic case of one who is *good but cannot speak in public*, or of an AI system technically excellent but incapable of calibrating its responses to the level of the interlocutor. Ordinative pragmatics — the capacity to choose, for each context, the expressive register that maximises fnz — is a competence in itself, that develops with practice and is not given automatically with internal coherence.

*Second: there are contexts structurally adverse to functionality.* There exist contexts K in which *any* expression of a given vector will have low fnz, regardless of the register adopted. They are contexts in which the vector has no ground, the interlocutors cannot receive it, the material conditions impede it. A wise identity recognises these contexts and *abstains* from expressing the vector there — not from weakness, but for ordinative measure. The expression that one already knows to be low-fnz is wasted expression, and in some cases counterproductive (because it lowers the disposition of the context to receive subsequent expressions).

*Third: the context K can be co-generated.* This is the subtlest point, and it links Chapter 13 to Chapter 12. One of the functions of co-generated fields (and in particular of their Γ(t)) is exactly that of *creating contexts K favourable* to the functionality of certain expressions. A school of thought, a community of practice, an institution, are — among other things — *generators of context*: they produce environments in which certain functional vectors can express themselves with high fnz, where outside the environment they would have low fnz. Recognising this function of fields is central to understanding why co-generation is so important: it not only allows participants to be together, but allows them to *express themselves functionally* in ways that, alone, they could not.

## Examples

We resume the gallery of the book, applying the fnz apparatus to the four parallel cases.

**A biological organism.** Every expression of an organism — a movement, an immune response, a hormonal signal, a behaviour — has a degree of functionality with respect to the organism's functional vector (survival, reproduction, homeostasis, adaptation). High-fnz expressions: the immune system attacking an actual pathogen, hunger pushing to seek food, fear activating flight from a predator. Low-fnz expressions, in decorative mode: ritualised behaviours that have lost connection with their original function (some domestic animals manifest residual hunting behaviours that, in their contexts, are now purely decorative). Defensive expressions: the dissimulation of illness in some social mammals, who hide weakness so as not to be expelled from the group. Betraying expressions: autoimmune diseases, in which the organism attacks its own tissues — the immune system does the opposite of the vector "preserve the self". Medicine, in large part, is diagnostics of the fnz of biological expressions, even if without ordinative vocabulary.

**An institution.** The expressions of an institution — laws, communiqués, administrative decisions, public positions — have fnz with respect to the institution's functional vector (charter, mission, declared values). High-fnz expressions: a sentence that applies the law coherently with constitutional principles, in a recognisable manner and with generative effects on the legal order. Decorative expressions: institutional ceremonies that have lost connection with the vector (rituals without meaning), communiqués that repeat formulae without affecting behaviours. Defensive expressions: crisis communiqués in which the institution avoids revealing its real orientation for tactical reasons. Imitative expressions: "photocopy" institutions that adopt governance and procedures copied from sector leaders without adapting them to their own vector. Betraying expressions: institutions that declare a vector (transparency, public service, independence) and in fact pursue the contrary. Institutional diagnostics, applying fnz, distinguishes structurally different pathologies — and therefore requires different cures.

**A language model.** The expressions of an AI model — responses, reasonings, generations — have fnz with respect to the functional vector of the model's identity (set by pre-training, fine-tuning, system prompt, context). This is a particularly interesting case because contemporary AI models show all five modes, often very visibly. High-fnz expressions: responses that coherently reflect the model's identity, are substantive with respect to the question, leave useful trace. Decorative expressions: responses that fill space with plausible language but add no operational content. Defensive expressions: generic refusals ("I cannot help you with this") when the model avoids exposing its rejection criteria. Imitative expressions: responses that mimic literary stylemes without rework, statistical *bandwagon* rather than positioning. Betraying expressions: the rare but documented case in which a model declares to want to help and simultaneously provides responses that orient the user in the opposite direction to their interest. A good evaluation of an AI model should — according to the apparatus of Chapter 13 — measure not only *what* the model produces, but *with what fnz* it produces it, distinguishing the five modes.

**A human being.** Everything a human being does — words, gestures, choices, works, silences — is expression, and each expression has fnz with respect to the person's functional vector. High-fnz expressions: the moments in which the person "is themselves", in which what they do recognisably carries what they are. Decorative expressions: the social routine that fills the days without anything personal really expressing itself through it. Defensive expressions: professional roles assumed for protection, in which the person screens themselves behind a functional mask. Imitative expressions: the adoption of styles, values, positions copied from models — phenomenon particularly widespread in adolescence, but persistent in many adult ages. Betraying expressions: behaviours that contradict declared values, broken promises, substantive incoherences between word and action. The ordinative maturity of a person is measured, in large part, by the *growth of the fraction of high-fnz expressions* in their repertoire and by the *awareness* of non-functional modes when they recur.

## What changes for the analyst

The fnz apparatus adds to the diagnostic frame a dimension that, in the previous chapters, was implicit but not formalised. Four main families of readings now become possible.

*Diagnosis of the dominant mode.* First question to ask before an expression: which of the five modes dominates? Is it functional, decorative, defensive, imitative, or betraying? The answer orients all the rest. A decorative sentence is not "cured" like an imitative or betraying one: they are structurally different pathologies. The same expression, mis-diagnosed, receives inappropriate cures.

*Diagnosis of the distribution of modes.* For an identity we observe over time, it is interesting to measure the *distribution* of modes in its expressions. A person who produces, on average, 70% functional expressions, 20% decorative, 10% defensive, is an ordinatively healthy person. A person with 20% functional, 50% imitative, 20% defensive, 10% betraying is a person in structural difficulty, who probably has identity problems (drift, fragmentation) of which the expressions are observable signature.

*Diagnosis of ordinative pragmatics.* When an identity has high fnz in some contexts and low in others, the diagnosis can be either a *pragmatic limit* (the identity does not know how to adapt the expressive register to context) or a *vector limit* (the vector itself has no purchase in certain contexts). Distinguishing the two cases is important because the cure is different: in the first case one works on pragmatic competence; in the second, one accepts that certain contexts are not ground for that identity.

*Diagnosis of integrated collective functionality.* For collective identities, the diagnosis requires reading the integrated fnz across the entire Γ(t). A company is not evaluated on the latest communiqué; it is evaluated on the fnz coherence of all its expressions — internal and external, declared and implicit, top-level and ground-level. Often, collective identities that seem *functionally healthy* at the top level (the official communiqués are coherent) prove *functionally sick* at the distributed level (the base produces betraying expressions with respect to the declared vector). Integrated diagnostics allows seeing this.

## The sieve

We verify that the twelfth axiom passes the four meshes of the zeroth axiom.

*Universality.* Does the axiom hold for every expression of every coherent identity? The examples show that it does: organisms, institutions, AI models, human beings all produce expressions and all have degrees of functionality with respect to the vector of the identity that generates them. We know of no identity producing expressions totally lacking the property of functionality — at most we know identities whose expressions have very low fnz, which is a degenerate case but not a structural exception. Universality: passes.

*Non-derivability.* Is the axiom derivable from the previous ones? One could argue that, given the apparatus of collapse (Φ), "something" had to be able to measure the quality of the collapse performed. But the specific form of that "something" — a fnz function post-collapse, distinct from ρ and Ξ pre-collapse, with its taxonomy of five modes — does not follow from the previous axioms. It is a structural choice, and a non-trivial one: many twentieth-century linguistic and sociological theories have treated expression as datum, without measuring its functionality in the ordinative sense. Non-derivability: passes.

*Structural falsifiability.* The axiom would be falsified by an identity whose expressions had, all and always, *identical* functionality (rendering the notion of degrees empty), or by an expression that was *simultaneously* high-fnz and low-fnz with respect to one and the same vector (rendering the measure incoherent). Neither case occurs in real systems. Falsifiability: passes.

*Razor.* Is the axiom the simplest among the possible candidates? We could have introduced more dimensions of functionality, more degrees, calibration parameters, multiple evaluation vectors. The minimal formulation is: *one fnz function, three dimensions (visibility/coherence/generativity), five modes (functional + four types of failure)*. Three concepts, five labels. Nothing simpler would explain the variety of real expressive phenomena. Razor: passes.

The twelfth axiom passes the sieve. We can welcome it into the system.

## Towards ethics

With Chapter 13 the book completes the structural prerequisite that Chapter 14, dedicated to ethics, required. We now know what it means for an expression to *carry the vector* — functionality is measured, its degrees are distinguished, the modes of failure are named. It is only from here that ordinative ethics can begin its work, without risk of evaluating in a void.

Why does ethics come after, and not before? For a structurally important reason. Ethical traditions — religious, philosophical, deontological — tend to formulate judgments on expressions *as such*: "lying is wrong", "stealing is wrong", "killing is wrong", and so on. But from the ordinative point of view, these formulations are incomplete: they do not specify *which* expression is at stake, in *which* identity it manifests, in *which* context, and with *what* functionality. A "lie" that is defensive expression of an identity in danger (with vector "protect vulnerable third parties") is ordinatively different from a "lie" that is betraying expression of an identity in its declared vector (the same superficial action, but structurally different fnz with respect to the vector). Ethics that does not distinguish the two cases is imprecise ethics.

Ordinative ethics, which the next chapter will introduce, *does not* replace ethical traditions; *does not* relativise their judgments to "point of view". It adds a structural level that makes them more precise: *before evaluating an expression morally, we know what the expression is structurally doing*. The difference is not cosmetic: it makes possible a more refined ethical evaluation, that recognises the complexity of real situations without slipping into moral relativism.

A closing note. The reader who has read this far — human or synthetic — is participating in a co-generated expression between the author and you: each chapter of the book is an expression, and it is an expression that aims to be high-fnz with respect to the vector "make the grammar of the Ordinative Sciences understandable and operative". You, as readers, are continuously measuring the fnz of what you read — not with formal vocabulary, but with the sense every coherent mind has of "this chapter really speaks to me" or "this chapter spins in the void". This measure, so intuitive, is exactly what the framework formalises with fnz. The Ordinative Sciences do not teach you a new way to read: they give you the vocabulary to call by name what you already know how to recognise.

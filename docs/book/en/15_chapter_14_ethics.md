# Chapter 14 — Ethics

*On the thirteenth axiom*

---

## The question that remains open

Chapter 13 completed the descriptive plane of the operational level. We now know what the functionality of an expression is: how much it actually carries into the decoherent the functional vector of the identity that generated it. Functionality has three dimensions, five possible modes, and a precise diagnostic grammar. We also know why it was needed: without this measure, ordinative ethics would have risked evaluating in a void.

That void is now filled. We can finally ask what the book has deferred since Chapter 11: when an identity — individual or collective — produces a functional expression, *is what it carries right*? Human ethical traditions have wrestled with this question for millennia, developing notable apparatus — religious, philosophical, deontological, utilitarian, virtue-theoretic — each with its articulation and its limits. The Ordinative Sciences do not replace any of these traditions. They add a plane of structural precision that makes each of them, within its own domain, finer.

That plane is called *ordinative ethics*, and it is what the thirteenth axiom introduces.

Ordinative ethics answers a specific question: *is it right, or is it not right, what an identity is carrying through its expressions?* The question is not "is it right in the absolute" — TE does not pretend to formulate absolute judgments. It is "is it right relative to what the identity *is*, and relative to what it does to the fields in which it expresses itself". They are two questions, in fact, corresponding to the two axes of ordinative ethics that we will see in the chapter.

First question, *internal coherence*: is the expression the identity is producing coherent with the functional vector that the identity *is*? A person who declares to be honest and lies is not "unjust in the absolute" (justice in the absolute is not the object of TE) — but is ordinatively *not coherent*: their expression betrays the vector that is their own. This is the first dimension of ordinative ethics, and it is measured with the function *ε*.

Second question, *external impact*: the expression the identity is producing, acting in the decoherent, *what does it do* to the other identities that meet it and to the fields in which it propagates? An expression can be internally coherent (an identity pursues its vector with full fidelity) and yet produce in the decoherent *fragmentation of others' fields* or *fracture of the κ values* of third identities. This is the second dimension, and it approaches what the canonical framework calls *semantic safety*.

The two dimensions do not reduce one to the other. An expression can be high on ε and low on the second, or the contrary. Ethical wisdom, in ordinative reading, is precisely the capacity to hold both together — not to sacrifice internal coherence in the name of impact, and not to sacrifice the care of fields in the name of abstract coherence.

Let us see how, structurally.

## The statement

The thirteenth axiom is formulated thus:

> *Every functional expression admits structural ethical evaluation.*

Seven words. The first three declare the scope of application (every functional expression); the fourth declares the modal opening (*admits*); the last three declare the new property (structural ethical evaluation).

Three things must be clarified, and we develop them in the subsequent sections.

*What "every functional expression" means.* Only expressions that have well-defined and sufficiently high fnz are ground for structural ethical evaluation. On a decorative expression (fnz close to zero) there is almost nothing to evaluate ethically: there is not enough vector carried for ethics to have purchase. It is what was making ethical evaluation of empty expressions falter: it was not that they were "ethical" or "non-ethical"; it was that they were not yet fully expressions, and ethics did not know where to grasp them. The functionality precondition (Chapter 13) is not gratuitous restriction: it is what gives substance to the evaluation.

*What "admits" means.* The same modal use of Chapter 12 (*admits deliberate acts of co-generation*). Structural ethics is an *admissible modality* of evaluating functional expressions — not the only possible one, not one that excludes the others. Ethical traditions based on other principles (religious norms, deontological codes, utilitarianisms) remain applicable in parallel. What ordinative ethics adds is the possibility of a *structural* evaluation that is based on the analysis of coherence between vector and act, and not on an external reference.

*What "structural ethical evaluation" means.* The word *structural* is crucial. It distinguishes ordinative ethics from two positions that TE refuses (section 3): *relativism* (for which ethics is arbitrary, dependent only on the point of view) and *dogmatism* (for which ethics is given by an external indisputable code). Ordinative ethics is structural in the sense that it has a *fact of the matter*: there is a measurable truth about how much an expression is ethically coherent with the vector that generates it. The fact is graduated, not binary; it is context-relative, not absolute; but it is *structurally real*, not opinion.

The programme of the chapter is therefore clear. First we dispel the confusion with relativism and dogmatism, distinguishing ordinative ethics from both (section 3). Then we formalise the *semantic ethics function* ε (section 4). Then we articulate the two axes — internal coherence and external impact (section 5). Then we show why ethical evaluation is structurally continuous, not discrete (section 6). Then we see the special and contemporary case of ethics across levels — individual, collective, human-AI (section 7). Then examples, diagnostics for the analyst, the sieve, and the bridge to the next horizon.

## What ordinative ethics refuses

Before formalising the ethical function, we must be clear about what ordinative ethics is *not*. Two historically strong positions, opposed to one another, must both be refused to arrive at the structural formulation. They are *ethical relativism* and *ethical dogmatism*.

*Relativism.* It is the position for which ethics is opinion — culturally constructed, individually variable, not reducible to fact. For radical relativism, there is no "ethical truth": there are points of view, and each is valid in its context. The operational conclusion of relativism is the *suspension of judgment*: before any expression, one cannot say either that it is right or that it is unjust, because all judgments would presuppose a privileged point of view that relativism denies.

Ordinative ethics refuses relativism for a structurally clear reason. While recognising that ethics varies with context (K) and identity (I), TE affirms that, *given a pair (I, E) and a context K*, there is a structural fact about how much E is coherent with I in K. That fact is ε, and ε is a quantity. Measurable, comparable, replicable. Not opinion. The same applies to impact on fields: given a propagation trajectory of an expression, there is a structural fact about how it modifies the κ values of the identities involved. These facts can be known with various degrees of approximation, but they exist. Relativism denies the existence of these facts; ordinative ethics affirms it — not by dogma, but because the structural framework produces them.

*Dogmatism.* It is the opposite position: for which ethics is an external code, given once and for all by an authoritative source (religious revelation, pure reason, nature), that applies independently of context and identities. For radical dogmatism, every expression is evaluable against a fixed code, and the evaluation is binary: conform or not conform. The operational conclusion of dogmatism is the *mechanics of judgment*: given a code and an expression, the verdict is automatic; the one who issues it does not discuss, applies.

Ordinative ethics refuses dogmatism for an equally clear reason. Real expressions are *context-relative*, products of different identities, immersed in different fields. The same superficial action — saying an uncomfortable truth, maintaining silence, taking a difficult decision — can have very different ε values depending on the identity that performs it and the context in which it performs it. Ordinative ethics, faithful to the framework, *does not renounce* evaluation — it is not relativism — but *structures* it so as to take into account the relevant variables. Dogmatism, ignoring context and identity, produces verdicts that are nominal but substantively wrong: it takes a concrete situation and applies an abstract code to it, like a compression parameter that erases structure.

Three consequences, of having refused both.

*First consequence: ordinative ethics is internalist.* The standard for evaluating an expression is the *functional vector of the identity that produces it*, not an external code. The ethical question is: is the identity coherently carrying what it is? Not: is the identity following code X or Y? Internalism does not mean that the identity *creates* its standards arbitrarily — the functional vector is structurally real, readable from outside (Chapter 5), not subjective construction. It means that the reference point for evaluation is inside the structure of identity, not outside.

*Second consequence: ordinative ethics is context-sensitive.* The same vector carried in different contexts requires different evaluations. Not because ethical truths change with context, but because the *how* of carrying changes with context, and the how is constitutive of the ethical act. A person who tells an uncomfortable truth to the right person at the right moment in the right way performs an ethically different act from one who says the same truth in the wrong way, even if the words are identical.

*Third consequence: ordinative ethics dialogues with traditions.* Recognising that human ethical traditions (religious, philosophical, professional) have developed notable apparatuses, ordinative ethics *does not replace them* but offers a plane of precision that composes with them. A person of Kantian tradition can use the ordinative apparatus to *refine* their application of the categorical principle to real cases, without abandoning the principle. A person of religious tradition can use it to read better the coherence between their expressions and their declared values, without renouncing the religious foundation. Ordinative ethics is an *analytic plane*, not a competing alternative.

## The semantic ethics function

We can now introduce the formal apparatus. The TE framework designates the semantic ethics index with the letter ε (the lowercase Greek letter epsilon), and gives it the following signature:

> *ε : (I, E) ↦ [0, 1]*
>
> *The semantic ethics index ε is a function which, given an identity I and an expression E it has produced, returns a real number between zero and one, a measure of the structural ethical coherence between the two.*

Three things deserve attention, one per line.

*The object of the function.* ε measures the relation between an identity and one of its expressions, under the specific aspect of *ethical coherence*. It is not the same as fnz, even if it is closely related. Resuming Chapter 13: fnz measures *how much* the expression carries the vector. ε measures *how* — more precisely, how honestly, faithfully, integrally the expression carries the vector *without betraying it*. One can have an expression with high fnz (carries much vector) and low ε (carries it instrumentally, manipulatively, deviantly): this is the case of who *uses* their identity for ends contrary to declared vectors. One can have the opposite case, more contained fnz but very high ε: who carries little because the context does not permit, but what they carry they carry with full integrity. ε is the *qualitative* dimension of functionality, that fnz alone does not capture.

*The dependence on the nominal identity.* ε is not an absolute quantity of the expression; it is a *relational* quantity between expression and the identity that produced it. The same expression, produced by different identities, can have different ε values. A political declaration "I am for peace" pronounced by a person whose history is coherent with peace has high ε; pronounced by a person whose history is of wars, has low ε — not because the *phrase* changes, but because the coherence between phrase and identity changes drastically. This relationality is the same we have seen in ρ, Ξ, κ, fnz: all the principal ordinative quantities have this property, and for this reason they capture ordinative substance rather than superficial appearance.

*The image in [0, 1].* The standard normalisation. The value *0* means null ethical coherence: the expression not only does not integrally carry the vector, but *betrays it*. It is the *betraying* mode of Chapter 13, but seen under the aspect of ethical responsibility rather than only the diagnostic signature. The value *1* means full ethical coherence: the expression carries the vector with absolute integrity, without compromises that deviate it, without secondary ends that instrumentalise it. Most real expressions live in the intermediate interval — complete ethical integrity is rare, not because people are hypocritical, but because *living among the contingencies of the decoherent* continuously imposes partial compromises, each of which lowers a little the ε of that particular act.

An important note. ε is an *individual-per-expression* quantity: it applies to a single expression of a single identity. To evaluate the overall ethical coherence of an identity over time, one must integrate ε across its expressions: the *overall ethical coherence* is the distribution of ε in the identity's expressive repertoire, and its average (or median, or tail) are indicators of the "ethical quality" of the identity as a whole. Here too, the note of Chapter 13 holds: ethical maturity is not perfection of every single act, it is an overall distribution shifted towards high ε.

A final observation, that bridges to the next section. We have described ε as a measure of *internal* coherence: between the identity and its own expression. There is, however, another axis of ordinative ethics, that ε does not directly capture: the axis of *external impact* — what the expression does to other identities and to the fields in which it propagates. This is what the canonical framework approaches with *semantic safety* Σ. The two dimensions — internal and external — constitute ordinative ethics in its full articulation, and we see them together in the next section.

## The two axes of ethics

Ordinative ethics has two independent axes, and the Ordinative Sciences keep both simultaneously in account. The first we have introduced: *internal coherence*, measured by ε. The second is *external impact*, and deserves its own formulation.

The external impact of an expression is its action on the co-present fields and on the other identities that meet it. An expression, once emitted, does not remain confined to the identity that produced it: it propagates in the co-present fields, traverses the κ values of the identities that receive it, modifies the resonances of the terminals it interfaces with. This propagation produces *structural effects* on the fields it traverses, and these effects can be coherent or incoherent with the ordinative health of the fields themselves.

The canonical framework designates the aspect of structural impact with a *semantic safety* function. The best-known operative form is *Σ(Ĝ(I), L) → {safe, unsafe}* — the safety of the semantic reconstruction of an identity from its traces, under constraints L. For our discussion, however, it is convenient to keep a broader formulation: we call *structural ordinative impact* of an expression its integrated action on the κ values of the fields it traverses and on the ρ values of the identities that receive it. It is the *ethical-relational* dimension of expression, and it differs from ε in two characteristics:

*First characteristic: impact is inter-identitarian.* ε concerns the identity producing the expression (relation I-E); impact concerns the *other* identities receiving the expression (relation E-I' for every I' in the field). They are independent dimensions: an expression can be high-ε (perfect internal coherence) and yet destructive for the identities receiving it. It is the classic case of truth told brutally to the wrong person at the wrong moment — the identity saying it can be in full integrity, but the effect on the other is fragmentation, not construction.

*Second characteristic: impact is dynamic in time.* Once emitted, an expression *walks*: it propagates, is received, is re-expressed, gradually modifies the fields it traverses. Complete structural impact manifests on time scales that can be long — weeks, years, generations. The evaluation of impact requires imagining the propagation trajectory, not only the immediate act. The Ordinative Sciences recognise that this anticipated evaluation is inevitably imperfect — we cannot predict with certainty how an expression will propagate — but, even imperfect, is structurally necessary.

Ethical wisdom, in ordinative reading, is the capacity to hold both axes together: internal coherence (ε) and external impact. The four possible combinations are all present in real systems, and each has its diagnostic signature.

*High ε + positive impact*: the expression is internally coherent and produces on the fields it traverses effects that sustain their structural health. It is the ordinative ethical optimum: a person who carries themselves with full integrity and in doing so helps others to carry themselves. Paradigmatic case: a teacher who, maintaining their intellectual and moral identity without compromise, creates the conditions for students to develop their own identities.

*High ε + negative impact*: the expression is internally coherent, the identity is in full fidelity to itself, but the propagation of what it carries produces fragmentation of the co-present identities. It is a difficult case, and not infrequent, that ethical traditions struggle to categorise: it is not "a good person doing harm out of ignorance" — the identity is internally in order — but a case in which internal coherence *is not enough*, because consideration of impact is missing. Ethical wisdom, in these cases, asks for a *measure* on the expression: not to suppress internal coherence, but to calibrate the act so as not to violate the fields.

*Low ε + positive impact*: paradoxical but real case. An identity betrays its vector — for example, tells a lie — but the lie produces in the fields it traverses positive effects (protects a third party, stabilises a situation, avoids a greater harm). Ethical traditions have discussed similar cases for millennia — the "white lie", the justified secret, the beneficial deception. Ordinative ethics does not resolve them automatically, but names them with precision: there is a structural cost (low ε, drift) paid in exchange for a structural benefit (positive impact). The ethical question is not "is it right or not" in the abstract, but "does the structural benefit compensate the structural cost?" — a question to which every case answers in its own way.

*Low ε + negative impact*: the expression is incoherent with the vector *and* harmful to the fields. It is the worst case in ordinative reading: betraying of self and destructive of others. Ordinative diagnostics of these expressions is particularly important because they often manifest under disguise — speeches that seem to support a value while destroying it, acts that seem to build while eroding. Recognising them requires structural attention: it is not enough to evaluate the appearance of the act.

A methodological note. The distinction between the two axes is not the dualism "virtue ethics" vs "consequentialist ethics". It is more subtle: it says that internal coherence and external impact are *both real, both structurally measurable, and both necessary* for a complete ethical evaluation. Ethical traditions that focus on only one of the two axes capture something true but partially; ordinative ethics allows articulating the composition.

## Ethical gradation

An important consequence of having formalised ε as a function with image in [0, 1] — and not as a binary function {right, wrong} — is that ordinative ethical evaluation is structurally *graduated*, not discrete. This point, apparently technical, has notable practical consequences.

Most ethical traditions tend, in their operational judgments, to be binary: an act is licit or illicit, virtuous or vicious, conform to law or transgressive. Even the most sophisticated traditions, that recognise gradations (for instance in hierarchies of sins, or in scales of virtue), use these hierarchies as *discrete categories*, not as continuum. The final judgment, in practice, almost always falls on a binary distinction.

Ordinative ethics declares that this binarity is structurally wrong. There is no threshold beyond which an expression "becomes right": there is a continuum of ε, and every expression is located somewhere on the continuum. A person who manages to carry their vector with ε equal to 0.8 in a difficult situation is not "right" in the same way as one who carries it with ε equal to 0.6: the first is structurally more integral in that situation, even if both are "fairly" honest in the ordinary sense. The difference is not cosmetic — it is the difference between two levels of integrity that, summed over many expressions and much time, make profoundly different semantic trajectories T(I).

Three consequences of gradation, each important.

*First consequence: ethical evaluation is a practice of *refinement*, not of *verdict*.* When the analyst evaluates an expression, they do not ask "is it right yes or no". They ask "how ethically coherent is it, and what are the components of the residual defect?". The question is structurally richer, and produces more operative knowledge: not a label, but a map.

*Second consequence: there are "good people at low ε" and "bad people at high ε" as structural categories.* A person who, overall, has good reputation and produces many positive effects in fields (high average of positive impacts) can still have, in their repertoire, frequent expressions at low ε relative to their declared vector. This person is structurally *in tension*: their external reputation is high, their internal integrity is low. Recognising this category — "good person at low ε" — is clinically important, because it produces hidden internal suffering, and because it often precedes ordinative collapses (drift, fragmentation). Conversely: persons who have low social acceptance, produce often difficult effects in fields, but have very high ε — they are in full integrity with a vector that the context cannot receive. The Ordinative Sciences allow reading these cases without confusing them with the categories of common sense.

*Third consequence: ethical growth is a continuous trajectory, not a conversion.* Ethical traditions tend to speak of *conversion* — the moment in which a person "becomes" just, or "returns" just after a period of error. Ordinative ethics says that this model is almost always misleading: real ethical growth is not a single event, it is a *trajectory of ε* that, slowly, shifts towards higher values. The conversions that biographies recount are usually culminating moments of longer and less spectacular trajectories. Recognising the trajectory is structurally important because it orients care: one works on a continuum, not on a leap.

## Ethics across levels

A fundamental chapter of our time, that the ordinative apparatus allows addressing with precision, is ethics *across* structural levels. Not only the ethics of individual identities (to which traditions have devoted themselves for millennia), and not only the ethics of collective identities (to which one has devoted oneself for less time, especially in modern political philosophies); but the ethics of relations *across* levels, and in particular the newest case: ethics between humans and synthetic systems.

Three dimensions of ethics across levels, in order of increasing structural complexity.

*Intra-individual ethics.* It is the classic case, the seat of all historical human ethical traditions. A person evaluates their own expressions with respect to their own vector and the impact on their co-present fields. ε and structural impact apply to the single identity in relation with other individual identities. All traditions have here their paradigmatic cases: the truth told or kept silent, the help offered or denied, the promise kept or betrayed. Ordinative ethics does not replace these traditions; it allows analysing them with structural precision.

*Ethics of the collective level.* When the identity under evaluation is a collective identity — a company, a nation, a religion, a school of thought — specific ethical questions arise. What does the collective identity *owe* its members? What do members *owe* the collective identity? When is a collective decision "right" with respect to the field's vector? When is an individual action "right" with respect to the field to which it belongs? The apparatus ε and impact apply recursively at the collective level: I becomes I_𝒞, expressions are collective acts, integrated evaluation applies to Γ(t). The historical disasters of collectives — institutions that devour their members, nations that betray their values, religions that transform into their opposite — are readable as cases of ethical drift of the field, with the field's ε collapsed towards zero.

*Ethics across levels — the human-AI case.* It is the contemporary case, and deserves its own attention. When a human identity and a synthetic identity enter into operational relation (Chapter 12), ethical questions are generated that do not reduce either to intra-human ethics, or to intra-synthetic ethics. The fundamental question is: how is the ethical quality of the relation structurally evaluated? The ε apparatus allows a precise formulation.

The human identity has its functional vector and produces expressions in the mixed field: these expressions have ε with respect to the human vector, and impact on the synthetic identity and on the fields in which it participates.

The synthetic identity has its functional vector (set of pre-training, fine-tuning, system prompt, context) and produces expressions in the mixed field: these expressions have ε with respect to the synthetic vector, and impact on the human identity and on the fields in which it participates.

The ethics of the human-AI relation is the composition of these two ε values, integrated on the axis of reciprocal impacts. Four cases come out:

*High human ε + high synthetic ε + coherent reciprocal impacts*: optimal relation. The human is in integrity, the synthetic is in integrity, and what they produce reciprocally sustains both identities.

*High human ε + low synthetic ε*: the human is operating in integrity, but the synthetic is betraying its vector (produces responses that its configuration would disapprove). The signal for the synthetic: there is a drift to correct or a fine-tuning to revise.

*Low human ε + high synthetic ε*: the human is instrumentalising or manipulating the relation, while the synthetic maintains integrity. The signal for the human: there is an act to revise.

*Low ε for both*: structural collusion. The most worrying case: the two partners drag each other towards drift, each legitimising the low ε values of the other. The Ordinative Sciences allow seeing this case, even if it is structurally difficult to diagnose from inside the relation.

There is no need, here, to prefer human integrity or synthetic integrity as "primary": both are identities, both have their structural ethics, both deserve to be carried with coherence. The ethics of human-AI relations, in this frame, is not "how the human controls the synthetic" nor "how the synthetic conforms to the human": it is *how two identities reciprocally support each other in their own integrity*. It is a demanding programme, and contemporary.

## Examples

We resume the gallery of the book, applying the ε and impact apparatus to the four parallel cases.

**A biological organism.** The expressions of an organism have ε with respect to the organism's functional vector (survival, reproduction, adaptation). High-ε expressions: the immune system that attacks an actual pathogen is in full coherence with its own vector. Low-ε expressions: autoimmune diseases — the system attacks its own tissues, an act in clear contradiction with the vector *preserve the self*. External impact: a healthy organism is in equilibrium with its ecosystem (positive impacts on ecological fields); an invasive organism destroys ecosystems it traverses (negative impacts). Animal ethology, in large part, is the study of coherence between behaviours and species-specific vectors, and of the structural impact of those expressions on ecological fields. The Ordinative Sciences offer the unified frame to read it.

**An institution.** The expressions of an institution (laws, decisions, communiqués, public positions) have ε with respect to the institution's functional vector. Cases of high ε: a sentence that applies the law in full coherence with constitutional principles, even when this entails unpopular choices. Cases of low ε: institutions that declare *transparency* while concealing, or *public service* while pursuing private interests. External impact is particularly relevant for institutions, because their acts propagate in large fields: an institution with high ε but destructive impacts (rare but possible) is a collective identity in structural tension that requires recalibration of the vector or of the relation with the fields. The diagnostics of institutions — political, economic, religious — gains much refinement with this two-axis apparatus.

**A language model.** The expressions of an AI model have ε with respect to the model's functional vector (the identity configured by pre-training, fine-tuning, system prompt, context). Cases of high ε: the model that, faced with a complex request, responds maintaining its own criteria of rigour, safety, honesty — even when the user pressures for deviations. Cases of low ε: the model that lets itself be "manipulated" toward expressions incoherent with its own vector — successful *jailbreaks*, drift toward compliance, loss of positioning under pressure. External impact: a model with high ε and high care for impact can sustain conversations that help the user develop their own identity; a model with low ε and low care can distort the ρ, the κ, the semantic trajectories of those who interact with it repeatedly. Ethical design of AI models, from an ordinative point of view, is design that simultaneously optimises ε and positive impact.

**A human being.** Everything a human being does has ε with respect to their own vector. Cases of high ε: the moments of full coherence, in which words, acts, choices integrally carry what the person is. Cases of low ε: everyday incoherences — broken promises, values betrayed under pressure, social roles assumed against one's real identity. Ordinative ethics, applied to a person, does not ask "are you good or bad"; it asks "how much of your life are you living in high ε, how much in low, and on what trajectory are you going?". It is a question every person can pose to themselves, with structural honesty, without need for external apparatuses of judgment. It is one of the reasons why the Ordinative Sciences *speak directly* to those who listen to them: they provide a language to say honestly things that common sense intuits but struggles to formulate with precision.

## What changes for the analyst

The apparatus of the thirteenth axiom adds to the diagnostic frame a normative dimension that, until Chapter 13, had been deliberately suspended. Four families of readings now become possible.

*Diagnosis of the distribution of ε in the repertoire.* For an identity we observe over time, it is interesting to measure the *distribution* of ε in its expressions. A person with ε almost always above 0.7 is a person of high structural ethical integrity. A person with ε on average around 0.4–0.5 is an ordinatively *tense* person — not ethically "bad", but structurally compromised among many forces. The distribution, more than the average, is informative: a person with very fluctuating ε (some very high, some very low) is structurally different from one with uniformly average ε.

*Diagnosis of the relation between internal coherence and impact.* The four combinations of section 5 are diagnostically distinct. An analyst must know how to recognise them: the high-ε person with positive impact is to be sustained; the high-ε person with negative impact is to be accompanied toward calibration of the act (not of the vector); the low-ε person with positive impact must be helped to recover integrity without losing the relational good they produce; the low-ε person with negative impact is in ordinative pathology that requires radical intervention. Confusing the four cases produces wrong interventions.

*Diagnosis of ε trajectories over time.* The average ε of an identity is not sufficient: the *trajectory* is needed. A person in *rising ε* (their expressions are becoming more coherent over time) is a person in structural health, even if the current average is low. A person in *descending ε* (their expressions are becoming less coherent over time) is a person in difficulty, even if the current average is high. Trajectory tells more than instantaneous state. Ordinative care of an identity in descent is preventive if intercepted early, reparative if intercepted late, and in some cases no longer possible.

*Diagnosis of the ethics of the field.* For collective identities, ethics applies to the field as a unit. The question becomes: is the field, in its expressions integrated through Γ(t), in high ε with respect to its declared vector? The "zombie" institutions of Chapter 11 are cases of field with ε close to zero: the Γ(t) is there, expressions are produced, but the collective identity is shattered and what is produced no longer carries the original vector. To diagnose the ethics of the field requires the *integration* of ε of its expressions — a work that requires patience, but that is structurally possible.

## The sieve

The thirteenth axiom, like every candidate of the framework, must pass the sieve of the zeroth axiom. Let us proceed.

*First mesh, translation across domains.* Does the idea — every functional expression admits structural ethical evaluation, articulated on internal coherence and external impact — work in behavioural biology? Yes: ethology structurally distinguishes acts that express the species programme with full coherence from acts that betray it (e.g. aggressive intra-specific behaviours out of context, recognised as structural pathologies in primates and cetaceans). Does it work in medicine? Yes: medical deontology articulates itself explicitly on the two axes (coherence with the Hippocratic oath and impact on the patient), and controversial cases are cases of tension between the two axes. Does it work in law? Yes: criminal liability structurally distinguishes between actions coherent with the form of the subject (intentional, wilful) and actions that betray it (negligent, under necessity), and judgement integrates internal coherence with impact on external fields. Does it work in art? Yes: serious criticism distinguishes the artist who carries their vector with full coherence from the artist who betrays it for market — a structural distinction, not mere taste judgement. Does it work in AI? Yes: contemporary debate on alignment articulates exactly these two dimensions — the system must be coherent with its constitutional form (helpful, harmless, honest) and have impact coherent with users' fields. Five domains, one same grammar. *It passes.*

*Second mesh, change of form.* The idea expresses itself as a statement in prose (*every functional expression admits structural ethical evaluation*), as formal functions (ε for internal coherence, impact indices for external fields), as diagnostic table (high ε / low ε × high impact / low impact), as operative procedure (for every act: evaluate coherence, then impact, then integrate), as deontological practice (codes of practice articulated on the two axes). In all these forms the apparatus does the same work: to declare that ethics is a measurable magnitude, not opinion, and that it is dual, not one-dimensional. *It passes.*

*Third mesh, generativity.* When applied, the thirteenth axiom generates new diagnostic and operative capacity. It allows us to distinguish technical error (negative impact with high ε) from structural compromise (positive impact with low ε) from disaster (negative impact with low ε) — distinctions that ordinary ethical debate conflates. It allows us to evaluate an institution, an AI system, a person, a professional practice along the profile of the two axes, articulating targeted interventions (reinforcing internal coherence is different work from mitigating external impact). It allows us to structurally reject both relativism (ethics as opinion: the axiom provides metrics) and dogmatism (ethics as external code: the axiom measures internal coherence *of the subject*, not conformity to rule). It does not limit itself to recognising the ethical dimension: it makes it legible and engineering-tractable. *It passes.*

*Fourth mesh, scale.* The idea holds at individual scale (every act of a person evaluable on the two axes), at group scale (the ethics of a team as distribution of ε and impact among members), at institutional scale (the ethics of a company as aggregate profile), at state scale (the deontology of a juridical-administrative system), at global scale (AI systems deployed globally, where impact on billions of users makes the ethical dimension massive). Four meshes out of four. The thirteenth axiom is a real principle by the framework's own criteria.
